Find Counterexamples and Test the Boundaries

Start here

“Every odd integer is prime” sounds plausible if you look only at 3, 5, and 7. The integer 9 meets the condition and fails the conclusion. One counterexample settles a claim about every member of a group.

Worked example

Take the statement “If positive integers aa and bb have no common factor greater than 1, and aa divides bcbc, then aa divides cc.” Test the role of the first assumption by dropping it and keeping everything else. Choose a=6a=6, b=4b=4, and c=3c=3. Then 6 divides 12 and 6 does not divide 3. The shared factor 2 in aa and bb supplies a factor that cc lacks.

This is boundary hunting. Choose one assumption to remove, keep the others, and search for a case that breaks the conclusion. If a candidate also violates a condition you meant to keep, discard it and try again.

Try it

  • Practice. Find a counterexample to “If a2>b2a^2>b^2, then a>ba>b” for integers. Find a restriction on both variables that repairs the implication.
  • Stretch. For positive integers aa and bb, prove that whenever they share a factor greater than 1, there is a positive cc with aa dividing bcbc and aa failing to divide cc.
