Counterexamples and Boundary Hunting

1. Core Idea

A theorem is only as strong as its weakest hypothesis. Boundary hunting is the systematic search for the case where a claim fails, and it serves three purposes:

  1. Refutation. One counterexample destroys a universal claim.
  2. Understanding. The counterexample shows exactly what a hypothesis was doing.
  3. Sharpness. The boundary case shows that a bound cannot be improved.

The methods are systematic, not creative:

  • Small cases. n=0,1,2n = 0, 1, 2. Empty set, singleton, trivial group.
  • Degenerate cases. Collinear points, zero-length segments, repeated roots, disconnected graphs, the zero vector.
  • Extremal cases. The maximum, the minimum, the boundary of the domain.
  • Hypothesis deletion. Remove one hypothesis at a time and look for a failure.
  • Minimal counterexample. If a counterexample exists, a minimal one exists; analyze its structure.

Principle signature. A claim is universal ("for all", "every", "always"), and you want to know whether it is true, how strong it is, or what its hypotheses are for.

2. First Principles

Universals fail at one point. ∀xP(x)\forall x\, P(x) is refuted by a single xx with ¬P(x)\neg P(x). This asymmetry is the whole reason counterexamples are powerful: the cost of refutation is one object; the cost of proof is an argument.

The failure localizes the hypothesis. If a claim fails when hypothesis HH is dropped, then HH is load-bearing. If it survives, HH is redundant. This is the empirical method for finding the minimal hypothesis set (→ Ch. 18).

Minimal counterexamples have structure. If a counterexample exists, then by well-ordering a minimal one exists (→ Ch. 10). The minimality is an extra hypothesis, and it usually forces a contradiction. This is the engine behind infinite descent and behind many impossibility proofs.

Sharpness is a property of bounds. A bound f≤Mf \le M is sharp if there is a case with f=Mf = M. A bound without a sharpness example is a statement about a possibly loose constant, and it may be improvable. Sharpness examples are as important as the bound.

Diagrams encode assumptions. A figure drawn with a point "inside" a triangle silently assumes an ordering that may fail. This is the mechanism behind the classical false proofs in geometry (all triangles isosceles, all angles equal), and it is why a case analysis must be driven by the algebra, not the picture.

3. Reasoning Process

  1. List the hypotheses. Each one is a candidate for deletion.
  2. Delete one at a time. For each, try to construct a counterexample. If you find one, the hypothesis is necessary; if you cannot, try to prove the theorem without it.
  3. Test the degenerate cases. The empty set, zero, one, the trivial object, the collinear configuration, the repeated root.
  4. Test the extremes. The maximum and minimum of each parameter; the boundary of each domain.
  5. Look for the minimal counterexample if a counterexample is believed to exist, and analyze its forced structure.
  6. Check sharpness. Exhibit the case where the bound is attained, or prove it is not.
  7. Re-examine the diagram. If the proof used a picture, redraw it in a different configuration and see whether the argument still applies.

4. Demonstrations

Demonstration 32.1 — A plausible divisibility claim, refuted

Claim. If a∣bca \mid bc, then a∣ba \mid b or a∣ca \mid c.

Counterexample. a=6a = 6, b=2b = 2, c=3c = 3: 6∣6=2⋅36 \mid 6 = 2\cdot3, but 6∤26 \nmid 2 and 6∤36 \nmid 3.

Diagnosis. The claim is Euclid's lemma, which requires aa to be prime (or coprime to one of the factors). The counterexample shows exactly which hypothesis is missing: 6=2⋅36 = 2\cdot3 splits the divisibility between the two factors.

Repair. If pp is prime and p∣bcp \mid bc, then p∣bp \mid b or p∣cp \mid c. The proof uses the fact that a prime has no nontrivial factorization — the very property the counterexample exploited.

What to notice. The counterexample did not just refute; it identified the missing hypothesis and suggested the correct theorem. This is the typical yield of a well-chosen counterexample: the failure mode is the hypothesis.

Demonstration 32.2 — Minimal degree is not enough for a Hamiltonian cycle

Claim (false). Every connected graph with minimum degree at least 22 has a Hamiltonian cycle.

Counterexample. Two triangles joined by a single edge (a "bridge"). Every vertex has degree 22 or 33; the graph is connected; but any cycle that visits all six vertices would have to cross the bridge twice, which is impossible in a simple cycle.

Diagnosis. Minimum degree controls local connectivity, not global. The bridge is a cut edge: removing it disconnects the graph, and a Hamiltonian cycle cannot contain a cut edge (removing one edge of a cycle leaves a path, which is connected).

Repair. A sufficient condition must control global connectivity: e.g. Dirac's theorem requires minimum degree ≥n/2\ge n/2, which forces enough edges to preclude bridges and much more.

What to notice. The counterexample is minimal in a precise sense: it is the smallest graph with a cut edge and minimum degree 22. When a conjecture fails, look for the smallest failure — its structure is usually the explanation.

Demonstration 32.3 — The diagram that assumed its conclusion

The classical false proof. "Every triangle is isosceles." Let ABCABC be a triangle. Draw the angle bisector of ∠B\angle B and the perpendicular bisector of ACAC; let them meet at EE. Drop perpendiculars from EE to ABAB and BCBC, and connect EE to AA and CC. Working around the figure, one "proves" AB=BCAB = BC.

The flaw. The proof assumes that EE lies inside the triangle and that the perpendicular feet lie between the vertices — i.e. it assumes an ordering of points on the sides. In a scalene triangle, the two lines meet outside the triangle, and the feet fall outside the segments. The equalities that the proof derives are still true, but they combine with different signs, and the conclusion does not follow.

The general lesson. The diagram encoded: (i) EE inside, (ii) feet between vertices, (iii) a particular order of the points on each side. None of these follows from the hypotheses. Hamming's observation applies: only by meta-reasoning — noticing that the conclusion is false — do we know the proof must be wrong, and then the search for the flaw begins.

What to notice. This is the most instructive false proof in geometry because the algebra is correct at every step. The error is entirely in the case assumption encoded in the figure. The defense is not "draw more carefully" — it is to derive the ordering from the hypotheses, or to prove the result for all orderings.

Demonstration 32.4 — Boundary hunting on a theorem

Theorem. A continuous function on a closed bounded interval [a,b][a,b] attains its maximum.

Boundary hunt. Which hypothesis can be deleted?

Deleted hypothesis Counterexample What the hypothesis does
Continuity f(x)=xf(x) = x for x<1x<1, f(1)=0f(1)=0 on [0,1][0,1]: no maximum (sup 11 not attained) Continuity prevents jumps that "miss" the supremum
Closedness f(x)=xf(x) = x on (0,1)(0,1): sup 11, no maximum Closedness keeps the limit point in the domain
Boundedness f(x)=xf(x) = x on [0,∞)[0,\infty): unbounded Boundedness prevents escape to infinity

Sharpness. On [0,1][0,1], f(x)=xf(x)=x attains its maximum at x=1x=1; the theorem is sharp in the sense that the maximum can be at an endpoint, so no stronger statement (e.g. "attained in the interior") holds.

What to notice. The table is the complete boundary analysis: every hypothesis is necessary, and each has a specific counterexample showing exactly what it prevents. This table is a better understanding of the theorem than the proof alone, because it tells you what happens in every neighboring theorem. When you learn a theorem, build this table.

6. Limitations

A failed search is not a proof. Not finding a counterexample in the cases you tried is weak evidence. The Pólya conjecture survived hundreds of millions of cases.

Counterexamples can be non-constructive. Some refutations show that a counterexample exists without producing one (e.g. by a cardinality or compactness argument). These are valid but less informative.

The minimal counterexample may be enormous. The minimal counterexample to a plausible conjecture can be far outside computational reach, which is why proofs are necessary even when the conjecture is "obviously" false.

Sharpness may be unattainable. Some bounds are not attained but are approached asymptotically; the correct statement is about a supremum, not a maximum (→ Ch. 11).

Diagrams are necessary and dangerous. Geometry without figures is impractical; geometry with figures is unreliable. The discipline is: use the figure to find the argument, use the algebra to verify the cases.

7. Applications

  • Mathematics. Counterexamples are the standard tool for sharpening theorems; the history of analysis is full of them (Weierstrass's nowhere-differentiable function, Cantor's set, the Banach–Tarski paradox).
  • Software testing. A test case is a search for a counterexample to "the program is correct"; boundary values and degenerate inputs are the highest-yield tests.
  • Engineering. Failure analysis is boundary hunting: find the operating point where the design fails, and understand which assumption it violates.
  • Law. A hypothetical is a counterexample to a rule; the reasoning that distinguishes it is the reasoning that limits the rule's scope.
  • Science. A single anomalous observation can refute a theory; the anomaly is the counterexample, and its structure is the clue to the better theory.
  • Statistics. Outliers and edge cases are the empirical counterexamples; investigating them is how models are improved (→ Ch. 29).

8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

  1. Delete each hypothesis of a theorem in turn and produce a counterexample or a proof without it.
  2. Build a boundary table for a theorem, as in Demonstration 32.4.
  3. Find the smallest counterexample to a plausible claim and explain its structure.
  4. Distinguish sharp from non-sharp bounds, and exhibit the extremal case.
  5. Detect a case assumption hidden in a diagram, and repair the proof by deriving the ordering.
  6. Use a counterexample to identify the missing hypothesis and state the corrected theorem.

Diagnostic question. "This theorem has three hypotheses. Which one is doing the work?" A learner who deletes them one at a time and produces counterexamples has the method.

AI Tutor Brief

  • Generate: (i) plausible false claims with a small counterexample; (ii) theorems to be boundary-tested (delete each hypothesis); (iii) false proofs with a hidden case assumption; (iv) sharpness questions for bounds.
  • Probe misconceptions: "no counterexample found, so it's true"; a diagram as a case analysis; sharpness as an afterthought.
  • Transfer distance: high. Test in algebra, analysis, combinatorics, and geometry.
  • Do not accept "it's obviously true" for any universal claim. Require the boundary hunt.
