Approximation, Asymptotics, and Scale

1. Core Idea

Most mathematical objects are too complicated for exact description at large scale. Asymptotics is the art of describing them by their dominant behavior: which term wins, how fast things grow, what the leading constant is, and how large the error is.

Three ideas:

  1. Order of magnitude. Two quantities are comparable if their ratio is bounded; one dominates if the ratio tends to infinity. This coarse classification is often enough to answer the real question.
  2. Dominant balance. In an equation with several terms of different sizes, the solution is usually controlled by a small number of dominant terms, except in thin regions (boundary layers) where the balance shifts.
  3. Perturbation. A small parameter ε\varepsilon lets you expand the answer as a series in ε\varepsilon; the first term is usually the answer, and the next term is the error estimate.

Principle signature. The problem involves a large parameter nn, a small parameter ε\varepsilon, a comparison of growth rates, a limit, or a quantity whose exact value is intractable but whose size is what matters.

2. First Principles

The hierarchy of growth. For large nn, logn≪na≪nb≪cn≪n!≪nn(0<a<b,c>1).\log n \ll n^{a} \ll n^{b} \ll c^n \ll n! \ll n^n \qquad (0<a<b,\ c>1). Each "≪\ll" means the ratio tends to 00. Knowing this hierarchy resolves most order-of-magnitude questions instantly, and it is worth memorizing as a structural fact rather than a list.

Big-O and its relatives. f(n)=O(g(n))f(n) = O(g(n)) means |f|≤C|g||f| \le C|g| for large nn. f=Θ(g)f = \Theta(g) means f=O(g)f = O(g) and g=O(f)g = O(f). f=o(g)f = o(g) means f/g→0f/g \to 0. f∼gf \sim g means f/g→1f/g \to 1. These are equivalence classes of growth, and they behave like a partial order, not like equalities — "OO" is not symmetric, and you cannot subtract OO-statements.

Dominant balance. In εx2+x−1=0\varepsilon x^2 + x - 1 = 0 with small ε\varepsilon, the naive limit ε→0\varepsilon \to 0 gives x=1x = 1. But the equation is quadratic and has two roots; the second one escapes to infinity as ε→0\varepsilon \to 0 (it is ≈−1/ε\approx -1/\varepsilon). The naive limit lost a root because the term εx2\varepsilon x^2 is not negligible when xx is large. The rule: a term that is small for typical xx may be dominant where the solution lives.

Integral comparison. For monotone ff, ∑k=1nf(k)\sum_{k=1}^n f(k) is comparable to ∫1nf(x)dx\int_1^n f(x)\,dx, with an error of order f(1)+f(n)f(1) + f(n). This converts sums into integrals and is the standard route to asymptotics of sums.

Why asymptotics is rigorous. An asymptotic statement is a theorem with an explicit error term: Hn=lnn+γ+O(1/n)H_n = \ln n + \gamma + O(1/n) is a precise claim about the size of the remainder. "Approximately" is not a proof; "=⋯+O(⋅)= \cdots + O(\cdot)" is.

3. Reasoning Process

  1. Identify the scale. What is large or small? nn, ε\varepsilon, tt, the dimension?
  2. Non-dimensionalize. Divide out the units and constants so that the small parameter is genuinely small (→ Ch. 27).
  3. Guess the dominant balance. Which terms should be comparable? Set them equal and solve for the scale of the solution.
  4. Check for lost solutions. Does the balance change somewhere (boundary, infinity, a degenerate point)? Those are the regions where the naive approximation fails.
  5. Compute the leading term. Then the next order, if you need an error estimate.
  6. State the result with an explicit error. f=g+O(h)f = g + O(h) — never just "f≈gf \approx g".

4. Demonstrations

Demonstration 22.1 — The factorial, bounded by elementary means

Claim. log(n!)=Θ(nlogn)\log(n!) = \Theta(n\log n).

Upper bound. Each of the nn factors in n!n! is at most nn, so n!≤nnn! \le n^n, hence log(n!)≤nlogn\log(n!) \le n\log n.

Lower bound. At least n/2n/2 of the factors are at least n/2n/2 (namely the factors kk with k≥n/2k \ge n/2), so n!≥(n2)n/2⟹log(n!)≥n2logn2=n2logn−n2log2=Ω(nlogn).n! \ge \left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{n/2} \implies \log(n!) \ge \frac{n}{2}\log\frac{n}{2} = \frac{n}{2}\log n - \frac{n}{2}\log 2 = \Omega(n\log n).

Hence log(n!)=Θ(nlogn)\log(n!) = \Theta(n\log n). ◼\blacksquare

What to notice. No Stirling's formula was needed. The two bounds used only the trivial estimates "all factors ≤n\le n" and "half the factors ≥n/2\ge n/2", and they already pin the growth rate. For order-of-magnitude questions, crude bounds usually suffice — and they are rigorous, unlike a remembered asymptotic formula.

The constant matters for finer questions: Stirling gives log(n!)=nlogn−n+O(logn)\log(n!) = n\log n - n + O(\log n), and the −n-n term is essential if you need the constant. But for Θ\Theta-classification, the crude argument is enough.

Demonstration 22.2 — Dominant balance and the lost root

Problem. Solve x2−2x+ε=0x^2 - 2x + \varepsilon = 0 for small ε>0\varepsilon > 0, asymptotically.

Naive limit. Setting ε=0\varepsilon = 0 gives x2−2x=0x^2 - 2x = 0, so x=0x = 0 or x=2x = 2. But the exact roots are x=1±1−ε=1±(1−ε2−ε28−⋯),x = 1 \pm \sqrt{1-\varepsilon} = 1 \pm \left(1 - \frac{\varepsilon}{2} - \frac{\varepsilon^2}{8} - \cdots\right), so x+=2−ε2+O(ε2),x−=ε2+O(ε2).x_+ = 2 - \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + O(\varepsilon^2), \qquad x_- = \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + O(\varepsilon^2).

Diagnosis. The root x+→2x_+ \to 2 behaves as the naive limit predicts. The root x−→0x_- \to 0, but its leading behavior is ε/2\varepsilon/2, not 00: the constant term ε\varepsilon and the linear term −2x-2x balance when x∼ε/2x \sim \varepsilon/2. At x=0x = 0 the quadratic term is negligible, but at x∼ε/2x \sim \varepsilon/2 it is negligible to leading order only, and it determines the correction.

What to notice. The naive limit is correct to leading order for one root and wrong for the other, because the balance of terms changes as x→0x \to 0. This is the prototype of a singular perturbation: the reduced problem (drop ε\varepsilon) has a different qualitative structure from the full problem (one root instead of two, or a lower-order equation). Recognizing it is the difference between an approximation and a blunder.

The same phenomenon appears in fluid mechanics (boundary layers), in differential equations (initial layers), and in algorithms (when a "negligible" term becomes dominant for small inputs).

Demonstration 22.3 — Integral comparison for a sum

Claim. Hn=∑k=1n1k=lnn+γ+O(1/n)H_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac1k = \ln n + \gamma + O(1/n), where γ≈0.5772\gamma \approx 0.5772 is Euler's constant.

Proof sketch. Since 1/x1/x is decreasing, ∫kk+1dxx≤1k≤∫k−1kdxx(k≥2).\int_{k}^{k+1}\frac{dx}{x} \le \frac1k \le \int_{k-1}^{k}\frac{dx}{x} \qquad (k \ge 2). Summing the left inequality from k=1k=1 to nn gives ln(n+1)≤Hn\ln(n+1) \le H_n; summing the right gives Hn≤1+lnnH_n \le 1 + \ln n. Hence Hn=lnn+O(1)H_n = \ln n + O(1). The refined statement that the O(1)O(1) term converges to a constant γ\gamma requires showing that Hn−lnnH_n - \ln n is decreasing and bounded below, which follows from the same integral comparison applied to the differences.

What to notice. The integral comparison gives the leading term (lnn\ln n) and the order of the error (O(1)O(1)) with no cleverness. The constant γ\gamma requires a separate convergence argument — the leading behavior is usually easy, the constant is usually hard, and the error term is usually hardest. Know which one your problem actually needs.

Demonstration 22.4 — Comparing growth rates without computing

Problem. For which nn is 2n>n1002^n > n^{100}?

Analysis. Take logs: nln2>100lnnn\ln 2 > 100\ln n, i.e. nlnn>100ln2≈144.3\frac{n}{\ln n} > \frac{100}{\ln 2} \approx 144.3. The function n/lnnn/\ln n is increasing for n>en > e and equals 144.3144.3 at roughly n≈1000n \approx 1000 (since 1000/ln1000≈1000/6.9≈1451000/\ln 1000 \approx 1000/6.9 \approx 145). So 2n>n1002^n > n^{100} for nn somewhat above 10001000.

Check. At n=1000n = 1000: log2(1000100)=100log21000≈100⋅9.97=997\log_2(1000^{100}) = 100\log_2 1000 \approx 100 \cdot 9.97 = 997, and log2(21000)=1000\log_2(2^{1000}) = 1000. So 21000>10001002^{1000} > 1000^{100} ✓.

What to notice. The crossover was found by solving an equation that has no closed form, using a rough numerical estimate. Asymptotic reasoning is quantitative but not exact: the goal is the right order of magnitude and the right crossover, not a closed form. The check at n=1000n=1000 confirms the estimate to within a factor of about 1.0031.003 in the exponent — good enough.

6. Limitations

Asymptotics is not exact. For small nn, the asymptotic answer can be qualitatively wrong. Always check the regime of validity.

Constants and error terms matter. Two algorithms that are both O(n2)O(n^2) can differ by a factor of 10001000. An asymptotic classification is the beginning of analysis, not the end.

Singular perturbations are easy to miss. When the small parameter multiplies the highest derivative or the highest power, the reduced problem has a different structure, and the naive limit loses solutions. This is the most common serious error in applied asymptotics.

Asymptotic expansions can diverge. Many series (e.g. for the error function) are asymptotic but not convergent: truncating at the right term gives an excellent approximation, but adding more terms makes it worse. "Asymptotic" and "convergent" are different concepts.

Dominant balance can be wrong. If two terms are comparable over the whole range, there is no single dominant term, and the scaling must be found by a different argument (self-similarity, matching, numerical experiment).

7. Applications

  • Algorithms. Complexity analysis is asymptotics; the choice of algorithm for large inputs is a growth-rate decision.
  • Physics. Perturbation theory, boundary layers, and renormalization are asymptotic methods; the small parameter is a coupling constant or a ratio of scales.
  • Statistics. Consistency and rates of convergence are asymptotic properties of estimators; the central limit theorem is the leading-order asymptotic.
  • Finance. The Black–Scholes formula has asymptotic regimes (deep in/out of the money) where different terms dominate; numerical stability depends on which regime you are in.
  • Engineering. Scaling laws (→ Ch. 27) predict how a design behaves as it is scaled up; asymptotic analysis tells you when the scaling law breaks.
  • Biology. Allometric scaling (metabolic rate ∝\propto mass3/4^{3/4}) is an empirical asymptotic law whose derivation is an active research question.

8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

  1. Classify functions by growth rate and justify the classification with a limit or a bound.
  2. Produce an upper and lower bound of the same order for a sum or product, using elementary estimates.
  3. Identify a singular perturbation and find the scaling of the lost solution.
  4. Use integral comparison to get the leading term of a sum, with an explicit error order.
  5. State an approximation with an explicit error term rather than "approximately".
  6. Determine the regime of validity of an asymptotic approximation and check it numerically.

Diagnostic question. "How big is n!n! compared to 2n2^n?" A learner who reaches for Stirling's formula immediately has memorized. A learner who bounds n!≥(n/2)n/2n! \ge (n/2)^{n/2} and concludes n!n! dominates has understood.
