Turn a Pattern Into a Conjecture

Start here

The rule n2+n+41n^2+n+41 gives 41, 43, 47, 53, and 61 at n=0,1,2,3,4n=0,1,2,3,4. Each is prime. Does the rule always give primes? At n=41n=41, the value is 41⋅4341\cdot43, which is composite.

Worked example

A conjecture is a precise mathematical claim you suspect is true and have yet to prove. “This looks prime often” leaves too much unspecified. “For every nonnegative integer nn, n2+n+41n^2+n+41 is prime” has a clear domain and a clear way to fail.

The failed guess suggests a new question. When is the expression divisible by 41? Since it is n(n+1)+41n(n+1)+41, divisibility by 41 is equivalent to 41 dividing n(n+1)n(n+1). A prime dividing a product divides at least one factor, so this occurs exactly when nn or n+1n+1 is a multiple of 41. A guess about all primes became a precise, provable statement about one factor.

Try it

  • Practice. State a precise conjecture about n2+nn^2+n for all integers and prove it.
  • Stretch. For a prime pp, characterize all integers nn for which pp divides n2+n+pn^2+n+p. Then find a composite pp for which the corresponding statement fails.

Check your work

Practice. n2+n=n(n+1)n^2+n=n(n+1) is even because one of two consecutive integers is even.

Stretch. For prime pp, pp divides the expression exactly when pp divides nn or n+1n+1. With p=6p=6 and n=2n=2, the product 2⋅32\cdot3 is divisible by 6, while neither factor is.
