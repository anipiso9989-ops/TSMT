1. Core Idea

A conjecture is a precisely stated mathematical claim that you believe but cannot yet prove. Conjecture formation is the disciplined transition from observation to claim:

observe→notice a pattern→find a mechanism→state precisely→test→refine\text{observe} \to \text{notice a pattern} \to \text{find a mechanism} \to \text{state precisely} \to \text{test} \to \text{refine}

The critical step is mechanism. A pattern without a mechanism is a coincidence with a good publicist. A pattern with a mechanism is a theorem waiting for a proof — and the mechanism usually tells you the proof strategy.

The second critical step is precision. "The primes are randomly distributed" is not a conjecture; it cannot be refuted, so it cannot be tested, so it cannot be research. "The number of primes in [x,x+h][x, x+h] is asymptotic to h/lnxh/\ln x when hh grows faster than x1/2+εx^{1/2+\varepsilon}" is a conjecture: it is quantitative, falsifiable, and connected to a mechanism.

Principle signature. You have data — numerical, combinatorial, structural — and a regularity that persists. Or you have a theorem and wonder how far it extends.

2. First Principles

Plausibility heuristics. Conjectures are generated by heuristics, and knowing the heuristics tells you how much to trust the conjecture:

Heuristic Form Reliability
Analogy "This is like that known case, so the same should hold." Medium — depends on the strength of the structural correspondence.
Simplicity "The simplest formula consistent with the data." Low — simplicity is a prior, not evidence.
Density/probability "There are so many chances for it to happen that failure is unlikely." Medium-high for existence, low for structure.
Extremal consistency "It holds at the extremes, so it should hold between." Medium — fails at phase transitions.
Symmetry "The answer must respect the symmetry of the problem." High when the symmetry is exact, misleading when it is broken.
Asymptotic matching "The asymptotic prediction agrees with the small cases." Medium — small cases can be unrepresentative.

Degrees of belief, honestly held. A conjecture is not a binary. Rank your confidence and record why: which heuristic supports it, which cases you checked, which mechanism you believe in. When it fails, the record tells you which heuristic was wrong, which is the actual learning.

Falsifiability is the entry ticket. A conjecture must specify what would refute it. If you cannot describe a potential counterexample, you have not stated a mathematical claim; you have stated a mood.

Statement quality determines attackability. Good conjectures are:

  • Quantified: "for all n≥Nn \ge N" with NN specified, or "for infinitely many nn", not "in general".
  • Sharp: they assert the best possible constant or the exact threshold, not a loose bound.
  • Local: they isolate one mechanism, so a failure localizes the error.
  • Connected: they imply, or are implied by, other statements — so progress on one is progress on many.

3. Reasoning Process

  1. Collect data systematically. Small cases, extremal cases, random cases, asymptotic cases. Not the cases that are easy — the cases that are informative.
  2. Look for invariants in the data. What stays the same as the parameter changes? Ratios, differences, residues, signs, growth rates.
  3. Propose a mechanism. Why would the pattern hold? A counting argument, a symmetry, an extremal configuration, a conservation law.
  4. State the conjecture precisely. Quantifiers, domain, sharpness, and the conditions under which it should fail.
  5. Test adversarially. Look for the smallest case that could break it, the boundary of the domain, the degenerate configuration.
  6. Refine. Either strengthen (the mechanism suggests more) or weaken (the failures suggest a hypothesis you missed).
  7. Record. The conjecture, the evidence, the mechanism, the confidence, and the falsification condition.

4. Demonstrations

Demonstration 31.1 — A pattern that breaks, and what the break teaches

Problem. Place nn points on a circle, draw all chords, and assume no three chords are concurrent inside the circle. Into how many regions is the disk divided?

Data.

nn 1 2 3 4 5 6
Regions 1 2 4 8 16 31

The tempting conjecture. "The number of regions is 2n−12^{n-1}."

The failure. At n=6n = 6 the count is 3131, not 3232. The conjecture is false, and it fails at the first case where the "no three concurrent" condition starts to matter in a non-generic way — five chords through a single interior point are excluded, and the count drops by one.

The correct formula. The number of regions is Rn=(n4)+(n2)+1.R_n = \binom{n}{4} + \binom{n}{2} + 1. Check: n=1n=1: 0+0+1=10+0+1=1 ✓; n=2n=2: 0+1+1=20+1+1=2 ✓; n=3n=3: 0+3+1=40+3+1=4 ✓; n=4n=4: 1+6+1=81+6+1=8 ✓; n=5n=5: 5+10+1=165+10+1=16 ✓; n=6n=6: 15+15+1=3115+15+1=31 ✓.

The mechanism. Use Euler's formula. Each interior intersection point comes from a choice of 44 points (the two chords), so Vint=(n4)V_{\text{int}} = \binom{n}{4}; the vertices on the circle are the nn points. Each chord is cut by the interior points on it, and counting edges gives EE. Then V−E+F=2V - E + F = 2 gives RnR_n. (The computation is a good exercise; the point here is that the formula has a derivation, not just a fit.)

What to notice. The 2n−12^{n-1} pattern held for five consecutive cases and failed at the sixth. Five cases is not evidence of a mechanism; it is evidence of a coincidence. The correct formula was found by asking why the count should have the form it does — and the "why" (Euler's formula, with (n4)\binom{n}{4} interior vertices) immediately produced the (n4)\binom{n}{4} term that the power of two could not explain.

This is the central lesson of conjecture formation: when you find a pattern, immediately ask what mechanism would produce it. If you cannot answer, treat the pattern as a coincidence and keep looking.

Demonstration 31.2 — Refining a conjecture: the Basel problem

Data. Partial sums SN=∑k=1N1/k2S_N = \sum_{k=1}^{N} 1/k^2:

NN SNS_N
10 1.549768
100 1.634984
1000 1.643935
10000 1.644834
100000 1.644924

First conjecture. The series converges (the partial sums are increasing and appear bounded).

Refined conjecture. The limit is π2/6≈1.644934\pi^2/6 \approx 1.644934. The identification is not from the digits — it is from the structure: the problem is about squares, the sum is over all integers, and π\pi appears in sums over lattices. Euler's derivation used the product expansion of sinx\sin x and matched coefficients.

What the refinement required. Not more digits — more structure. The numerical value told Euler what to prove; the product formula told him how.

What to notice. The conjecture went through three stages: existence (converges), value (a specific number), and mechanism (the sine product). Each stage was a separate claim with separate evidence. A conjecture that is only "the limit is about 1.6449" is not yet mathematics; it becomes mathematics when it is identified with a closed form that can be proved.

The practical lesson: when refining a conjecture, ask what would make this provable? A closed form, a recurrence, a generating function, an identity — these are the forms in which conjectures become theorems.

Demonstration 31.3 — A plausibility argument that is not a proof

Conjecture (Goldbach). Every even integer N>2N > 2 is a sum of two primes.

Plausibility heuristic. For large NN, the number of primes below NN is about N/lnNN/\ln N. If primes behaved like a random subset of the integers with density 1/lnN1/\ln N, the number of ways to write N=p+qN = p + q would be about N(lnN)2.\frac{N}{(\ln N)^2}. For N=109N = 10^9 this is about 109/(20.7)2≈2.3×10610^9/(20.7)^2 \approx 2.3\times10^6 representations. A failure would require every one of these to be absent, which is fantastically unlikely if the primes are even approximately random.

What this does and does not establish. It establishes that the conjecture is morally certain under a randomness heuristic. It does not establish the conjecture, because the primes are not random — they are deterministic, and the heuristic ignores all arithmetic structure (for instance, the parity of pp and qq is constrained: for even NN, both must be odd except in the case N=4=2+2N = 4 = 2+2).

What to notice. The heuristic is genuinely informative: it tells you where to look for a counterexample (nowhere, essentially) and why the conjecture is hard (the difficulty is in the arithmetic structure that the heuristic discards). It also tells you what a proof would need to do: it would need to control the arithmetic correlations that the random model ignores.

This is the correct use of plausibility arguments: not as evidence for belief, but as a guide to mechanism. A conjecture supported by a heuristic is a conjecture with a research program attached.

Demonstration 31.4 — Stating a conjecture so it can be attacked

Vague. "The primes are distributed randomly."

Why it is not a conjecture. It cannot be refuted: any finite pattern of primes is consistent with some notion of randomness. It also cannot be proved, because it has no precise content.

Precise (a version of the prime number theorem with an error term). "There is a constant c>0c > 0 such that for all x≥2x \ge 2, |π(x)−li(x)|≤cxexp(−clnx),\left|\pi(x) - \operatorname{li}(x)\right| \le c\, x \exp\left(-c\sqrt{\ln x}\right), where π(x)\pi(x) is the number of primes ≤x\le x and li(x)=∫2xdt/lnt\operatorname{li}(x) = \int_2^x dt/\ln t."

Why it is a conjecture. It is quantitative, it specifies the domain, it has a falsification condition (find an xx violating the bound), and it is connected to other statements (it implies the prime number theorem and is related to the Riemann hypothesis).

What to notice. The vague statement and the precise statement are "about the same thing", but only one of them can be worked on. Precision is not pedantry; it is the difference between a research program and a sentiment. When you find yourself believing something vague, the most valuable thing you can do is force it into a quantified statement — even if the statement is wrong, because a wrong precise statement can be refuted, and refutation is progress.

6. Limitations

Heuristics can be systematically wrong. The density heuristic fails when arithmetic correlations dominate (Goldbach's parity constraint is a trivial example; the failure of naive randomness for primes is the deep one). Every heuristic has a domain.

Patterns in small cases are unreliable. The Moser circle problem failed at n=6n=6; the Pólya conjecture held for hundreds of millions of cases and then failed. Small cases test the statement, not the mechanism.

Precision can be premature. Formalizing a conjecture before you understand the mechanism can lock you into the wrong statement. Iterate: vague → precise → test → refine.

Conjectures can be unfalsifiable in practice. A statement about numbers too large to compute is falsifiable in principle and not in practice. This is a real limitation on the empirical method, and it is why proofs matter.

Belief is not evidence. The strength of your conviction is not a reason for anyone else to believe the conjecture. Record the evidence, not the feeling.

7. Applications

  • Mathematics. The Riemann hypothesis, Goldbach, twin primes, and the Collatz conjecture are the canonical examples: precise, falsifiable, supported by massive evidence, and unproved.
  • Theoretical computer science. 𝐏≠𝐍𝐏\mathbf{P} \ne \mathbf{NP} is a conjecture with enormous evidence (no known algorithm, many failed attempts) and no proof; the conjecture's precise statement is what makes the research program possible.
  • Physics. Conjectures about the spectrum, about confinement, and about the string landscape are precise claims awaiting proof or refutation.
  • Data science. A hypothesis is a conjecture with a statistical falsification condition; the discipline of pre-registration is the discipline of stating it before looking at the data.
  • Engineering. A design conjecture ("this architecture will scale to 10610^6 users") is falsifiable by a load test; stating it precisely is what makes the test informative.

8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

  1. Generate a pattern from data and immediately ask what mechanism would produce it.
  2. State a conjecture with explicit quantifiers, domain, and sharpness.
  3. Give a falsification condition for any conjecture you state.
  4. Distinguish a plausibility heuristic from evidence, and say which heuristic you used.
  5. Refine a conjecture from existence to value to mechanism.
  6. Identify the case that would most likely break your conjecture, and test it.

Diagnostic question. "What would refute this?" A learner who cannot answer has not stated a conjecture. A learner who names the smallest likely counterexample has.

AI Tutor Brief

  • Generate: (i) data sequences with a tempting false pattern and a true formula; (ii) vague claims to be made precise; (iii) conjectures to be tested adversarially; (iv) plausibility heuristics to be evaluated (which are reliable here, and why?).
  • Probe misconceptions: "it holds for 10 cases, so it's true"; simplicity as evidence; treating a heuristic as a proof; unfalsifiable statements as conjectures.
  • Transfer distance: maximal. Every problem should require the learner to state something, not just solve something.
  • Do not reward a correct conjecture without a mechanism and a falsification condition.
