1. Core Idea

Fermi reasoning is the construction of a quantitative answer from rough, individually unreliable estimates, in a way that is accurate to within an order of magnitude.

It works because of two structural facts:

  1. Decomposition converts an impossible question into several easy ones. "How many piano tuners are in Chicago?" is unanswerable directly; "how many households, what fraction own a piano, how often is it tuned, how many tunings per tuner per day?" are each answerable to within a factor of two.
  2. Multiplicative errors partially cancel. If each of kk factors is off by a factor of 22 in a random direction, the product is off by roughly 2k2^{\sqrt{k}} — much less than 2k2^k. Working in logarithms makes this explicit: errors add, and independent errors add in quadrature.

Estimation is not a lesser form of computation. It is the only form of computation available when the data do not exist, and it is the primary defense against quantitative nonsense.

Principle signature. The question is quantitative but the data are unavailable, expensive, or disputed; or you need to check whether a claimed number is plausible before investing in a precise calculation.

2. First Principles

Logarithmic arithmetic. Work with powers of 1010. The useful anchors:

210≈103,e3≈20,π2≈10,10≈3.16.2^{10} \approx 10^3, \qquad e^3 \approx 20, \qquad \pi^2 \approx 10, \qquad \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16.

1 year≈π×107 s≈3.15×107 s.1 \text{ year} \approx \pi \times 10^7 \text{ s} \approx 3.15\times10^7\text{ s}.

Bounds beat point estimates. An upper bound and a lower bound that agree to within a factor of 1010 are usually enough. Bounds are also more robust: they are valid even when the point estimate is wrong.

The 70 rule. A quantity growing at p%p\% per year doubles in about 70/p70/p years. (Because ln2≈0.693\ln 2 \approx 0.693 and ln(1+p/100)≈p/100\ln(1+p/100) \approx p/100.)

Independent routes. Estimate the same quantity two ways, with different decompositions. If the two answers agree within a factor of 33, you have a real estimate. If they disagree by 10310^3, one decomposition has a structural error, and finding it is the valuable part.

Calibration. Estimation improves with feedback. Keep a record of estimates and actual values; the errors are usually systematic (you consistently overestimate one kind of quantity), and systematic errors can be corrected.

3. Reasoning Process

  1. Write the quantity with its units. "XX kilograms", not "XX".
  2. Decompose multiplicatively into factors you can estimate independently. Aim for 3–6 factors.
  3. Estimate each factor to one significant figure, with a range. Use anchors you know (a person is ~10210^2 kg, a year is ~3×1073\times10^7 s, a city block is ~10210^2 m).
  4. Combine in log space. Add the exponents; multiply the mantissas.
  5. Sanity-check with a second decomposition. If the two routes disagree by more than 10×10\times, find the error.
  6. State the answer as a range, with the dominant uncertainty named.

4. Demonstrations

Demonstration 26.1 — The mass of the atmosphere

Question. What is the total mass of Earth's atmosphere?

Decomposition. Surface pressure is the weight of the atmosphere per unit area: p=MgA⟹M=pAg.p = \frac{Mg}{A} \implies M = \frac{pA}{g}.

Inputs. p≈1.01×105Pap \approx 1.01\times10^5\ \mathrm{Pa} (known). g≈9.8m/s2g \approx 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}. Earth's radius R≈6.37×106mR \approx 6.37\times10^6\ \mathrm{m}, so A=4πR2≈4π×4.06×1013≈5.1×1014m2.A = 4\pi R^2 \approx 4\pi \times 4.06\times10^{13} \approx 5.1\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^2}.

Combine. M≈1.01×105×5.1×10149.8≈5.15×10199.8≈5.3×1018kg.M \approx \frac{1.01\times10^5 \times 5.1\times10^{14}}{9.8} \approx \frac{5.15\times10^{19}}{9.8} \approx 5.3\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{kg}.

Check. The accepted value is about 5.15×10185.15\times10^{18} kg. The estimate is within 3% — and it required no knowledge of the atmosphere's composition, temperature profile, or height.

What to notice. The estimate is accurate because the physics gave an exact relation (p=Mg/Ap = Mg/A), not because the inputs were precise. A good decomposition can turn a rough estimate into an accurate one. This is the ideal case: the structure does the work, and the numbers only need one significant figure.

Demonstration 26.2 — How many digits in 100!100!?

Question. Estimate the number of decimal digits of 100!100!.

Decomposition. The number of digits is ⌊log10(100!)⌋+1\lfloor \log_{10}(100!)\rfloor + 1. By Stirling's approximation, log10(n!)≈nlog10ne+12log10(2πn).\log_{10}(n!) \approx n\log_{10}\frac{n}{e} + \frac12\log_{10}(2\pi n). With n=100n = 100: log10(100/e)=2−log10e=2−0.4343=1.5657\log_{10}(100/e) = 2 - \log_{10} e = 2 - 0.4343 = 1.5657. So 100×1.5657=156.57,12log10(2π⋅100)=12log10(628.3)≈12(2.798)=1.399.100 \times 1.5657 = 156.57, \qquad \frac12\log_{10}(2\pi\cdot100) = \frac12\log_{10}(628.3) \approx \frac12(2.798) = 1.399. Total: 156.57+1.40=157.97156.57 + 1.40 = 157.97. Hence log10(100!)≈157.97\log_{10}(100!) \approx 157.97, so 100!100! has ⌊157.97⌋+1=158\lfloor 157.97 \rfloor + 1 = 158 digits.

Check. The exact value of 100!100! has 158 digits. ✓

What to notice. Stirling's formula is doing the real work, and the estimation is really an asymptotic calculation (→ Ch. 22). The distinction between estimation and asymptotics is one of intent: asymptotics asks for the growth law with an error term; estimation asks for a number to one significant figure. Here they coincide.

Note also the correction term: without 12log10(2πn)\frac12\log_{10}(2\pi n), the estimate would be 156.57156.57, giving 157157 digits — off by one, which matters when the question is "how many digits". When the answer is an integer near a boundary, the correction term is not optional.

Demonstration 26.3 — Detecting a nonsense claim

Claim. "Our new data center processes one billion customer transactions per day on a single server."

Check. One billion transactions per day is 10924×3600≈1098.64×104≈1.16×104 transactions per second.\frac{10^9}{24\times3600} \approx \frac{10^9}{8.64\times10^4} \approx 1.16\times10^4 \text{ transactions per second}. That is about 11,60011{,}600 transactions per second, i.e. about 8686 microseconds per transaction, sustained, on one machine, with no time for network, storage, or failures.

Verdict. Physically possible only for extremely simple transactions (an in-memory key-value update), and implausible for anything involving a database write, a network round trip, or a consistency protocol — each of which costs hundreds of microseconds to milliseconds. The claim is off by one to three orders of magnitude for any realistic workload.

What to notice. The check took thirty seconds and required no domain expertise beyond "a network round trip is ~10−410^{-4} s". The most valuable use of estimation is not producing numbers but rejecting them. A quantitative claim that survives a thirty-second order-of-magnitude check is worth taking seriously; one that does not is worth ignoring, regardless of the credentials of the source.

Demonstration 26.4 — Two routes, and what disagreement means

Question. How many cells are in the human body?

Route A (mass per cell). A typical human cell is about 10μm10\ \mu\mathrm{m} across, so its volume is about (10−5)3=10−15m3(10^{-5})^3 = 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{m^3}, and with density ≈103kg/m3\approx 10^3\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} its mass is about 10−12kg10^{-12}\ \mathrm{kg}. A 7070 kg human therefore contains about N≈7010−12=7×1013 cells.N \approx \frac{70}{10^{-12}} = 7\times10^{13} \text{ cells}.

Route B (cell count by type). Red blood cells are about 5×10125\times10^{12} per liter of blood and there are about 55 liters, giving 2.5×10132.5\times10^{13}; add roughly the same again for everything else, giving ∼5×1013\sim 5\times10^{13}.

Agreement. Both routes give ∼1013\sim 10^{13}–101410^{14}, consistent with the accepted value of about 3.7×10133.7\times10^{13} (bacterial cells add more, but they are much smaller).

What to notice. The two routes used different decompositions and agreed within a factor of 22. That agreement is what licenses confidence. A single route gives a number; two routes give an estimate. When the routes disagree, the disagreement is the finding: it locates the factor that one route got structurally wrong.

6. Limitations

Estimation cannot distinguish close alternatives. If two options differ by 20%, estimation is useless; you need measurement or computation. Estimation is for order-of-magnitude decisions.

Systematic bias does not cancel. Multiplicative errors cancel only if they are independent and symmetric. If you consistently underestimate a class of quantities (a common pattern), the errors compound. Calibration is the remedy.

The decomposition can be structurally wrong. A correct arithmetic combination of wrong factors gives a confident wrong answer. The second route is the defense.

Fermi reasoning can create false confidence. A number with a range looks more authoritative than a guess, but the range may be fictional. State the uncertainty honestly, and distinguish "I bounded this" from "I guessed this".

Some quantities are not decomposable. If you have no anchor for any factor, no decomposition helps. In that case, the honest answer is "unknown, and here is what it would take to know".

7. Applications

  • Engineering feasibility. Before designing a system, estimate its power, bandwidth, mass, and cost. If the estimate is off by 100×100\times, the design is dead and no detailed analysis will save it.
  • Business and policy. Market sizing, cost projections, and policy impact estimates are Fermi problems; the value is in identifying which factor dominates the uncertainty.
  • Science. Order-of-magnitude estimates decide which experiments are worth running and which theories are worth testing.
  • Medicine. Dosage, incidence, and risk estimates are order-of-magnitude reasoning with safety factors.
  • Software. Back-of-the-envelope capacity planning (requests per second, storage per user, latency budgets) is the standard first step in system design.
  • Personal decisions. Time, money, and energy budgets are Fermi problems; most errors come from ignoring a factor, not from misestimating one.

8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

  1. Decompose a quantity into 3–6 independently estimable factors and combine them in log space.
  2. Produce two independent routes to the same quantity and interpret their agreement or disagreement.
  3. Reject a quantitative claim by a thirty-second order-of-magnitude check.
  4. Use anchors (210≈1032^{10}\approx10^3, a year ≈3×107\approx 3\times10^7 s, a person ≈102\approx 10^2 kg) fluently.
  5. State an estimate as a range with the dominant uncertainty identified.
  6. Distinguish "bounded" from "guessed", and say which you have done.

Diagnostic question. "Is this number plausible?" A learner who reaches for a decomposition and a bound has the skill. A learner who says "it depends" has not.

AI Tutor Brief

  • Generate: (i) Fermi questions with no lookup available; (ii) implausible claims to be rejected with a calculation; (iii) two-route estimation tasks where the routes disagree; (iv) estimates requiring a correction term (Stirling, log corrections) where the naive answer is off by one.
  • Probe misconceptions: single-route confidence; ignoring units; treating a point estimate as exact; forgetting the correction term when the answer is an integer near a boundary.
  • Transfer distance: maximal. Use quantities from physics, biology, economics, and software.
  • Do not require precision. Grade the decomposition, the anchors, and the honesty of the range.
