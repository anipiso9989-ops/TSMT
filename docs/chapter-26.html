Estimate Before You Calculate

Start here

How many ping-pong balls fit in a classroom? Before hunting for an exact answer, get the scale. Break the question into quantities you can estimate and write the units next to each one. This is Fermi estimation.

Worked example

Take a classroom about 8 m long, 6 m wide, and 3 m tall. Its empty volume is about 144m3144\,\text{m}^3. Suppose each ball occupies roughly a cube 4 cm on a side in a loose grid. Each cube takes up about 0.043=0.000064m30.04^3=0.000064\,\text{m}^3. The quotient gives a little over two million balls in that grid model.

Check by another route. You could fit about 200 ball widths along the length, 150 along the width, and 75 along the height. Multiply to get 2,250,0002{,}250{,}000. This verifies the units and arithmetic. Both routes share the same packing assumption. To test that assumption, count how many balls fit in a small box and use the count per liter as a separate estimate.

Try it

  • Practice. Estimate how many one-liter bottles a school of 800 students could fill if each used about 0.6 liters of water each day. State the assumption about days.
  • Stretch. Give a plausible low and high daily estimate if the school has 750–850 students present and each uses 0.4–0.8 liters. Which range contributes the larger relative uncertainty?

Check your work

Practice. One school day gives 800⋅0.6=480800\cdot0.6=480 liters, or 480 one-liter bottles' worth.

Stretch. The stated ranges imply 300–680 liters. The per-student estimate varies by a factor of 2. Attendance varies by a factor of 850/750850/750, so consumption per student accounts for the larger relative variation.
