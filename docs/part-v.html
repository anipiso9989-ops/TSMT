Part V / Chapters 31–35

Independent Mathematical Research

On this page

  • Chapters in this part
  • Part V checkpoint

In Parts I–IV the problem was given. Here it is not. The final five chapters train the abilities that make a mathematician independent: forming conjectures, breaking them, experimenting, inventing problems, and judging which problems deserve a life.

The shift in standards

Up to here, correctness was the standard. From here, the standards are:

  • Productive wrongness. A precise conjecture that turns out false is more valuable than a vague claim that turns out true, because the failure localizes the mechanism.
  • Falsifiability. Every claim must come with a description of what would refute it.
  • Taste. Among infinitely many true statements, only a few are worth proving. Taste is the ability to tell which, and it is trainable — not by rule, but by exposure to many examples of both kinds.

Part V checkpoint

  1. State a conjecture with a mechanism, a falsification condition, and a sharpness claim. (Ch. 31)
  2. Build a boundary table for a theorem, deleting each hypothesis. (Ch. 32)
  3. Design an experiment, transform the data, and state a conjecture with a mechanism. (Ch. 33)
  4. Generate five transformed variants of a result and select one on the four criteria. (Ch. 34)
  5. Write the four fields for a problem you care about, and state your abandonment criterion. (Ch. 35)

If you can do these five, you no longer need this guide. You need problems.
