Dimensional Analysis and Scaling

1. Core Idea

Physical quantities carry dimensions — mass MM, length LL, time TT, and others — and any equation relating them must be dimensionally homogeneous: both sides must have the same dimensions.

This constraint is far stronger than it looks. It often determines the form of a physical law up to a dimensionless constant, without solving any equations. The procedure is the Buckingham Π\Pi theorem:

If a phenomenon depends on nn dimensional variables involving kk independent dimensions, then the variables can be combined into n−kn-k independent dimensionless groups, and the law relating them is a relation among those groups.

Scaling is the corollary: when all lengths are multiplied by λ\lambda, quantities transform as powers of λ\lambda, and the exponents are determined by the dimensions. This is how you predict how a design behaves when it is scaled up — and why some designs cannot be scaled up at all.

Principle signature. A physical law is sought; the variables are known but the equation is not; or a design must be scaled; or a quantity's dependence on size, speed, or temperature is at issue.

2. First Principles

Dimensions form a vector space. Each quantity has a dimension vector in the basis (M,L,T,Θ,…)(M, L, T, \Theta, \ldots). Multiplication adds vectors; powers multiply them; addition requires equal vectors. Dimensionless quantities have the zero vector.

Homogeneity forces the form. If y=f(x1,…,xn)y = f(x_1,\dots,x_n) and the dimensions are known, then ff must be such that the dimension vectors match. In the simplest case, ff is a monomial C∏xiaiC\prod x_i^{a_i}, and the exponents solve a linear system.

The Buckingham Π\Pi theorem, concretely. With nn variables and kk independent dimensions, there are n−kn-k dimensionless groups Π1,…,Πn−k\Pi_1,\dots,\Pi_{n-k}, and the law has the form Π1=Φ(Π2,…,Πn−k)\Pi_1 = \Phi(\Pi_2,\dots,\Pi_{n-k}) for some function Φ\Phi of n−k−1n-k-1 arguments. Dimensional analysis determines the groups but not the function Φ\Phi — that requires physics, experiment, or a deeper theory.

Scaling laws. If a system is scale-invariant (no intrinsic length scale), then the dimensionless groups are constant, and every quantity is a power law in the scale. The exponents are the critical exponents, and they are determined by dimensional analysis plus the assumption of scale invariance.

The limits. Dimensional analysis cannot determine dimensionless constants (2π2\pi, 1/21/2, the fine-structure constant). It cannot distinguish regimes (laminar vs turbulent) — the regime is set by the magnitude of a dimensionless group, not its existence. And it requires the correct variable list: omit a relevant variable and the analysis gives a wrong (but dimensionally consistent) law.

3. Reasoning Process

  1. List the variables that could plausibly matter, with their dimensions. Be generous at first, then prune by physical reasoning.
  2. Write the dimension matrix (rows = dimensions, columns = variables) and find its rank kk.
  3. Form the dimensionless groups. Solve for the null space: each null vector gives a group ∏xiai\prod x_i^{a_i}.
  4. Write the law as a relation among the groups.
  5. Determine the function by experiment, by a limiting case, or by a deeper theory.
  6. Check the scaling. How does each quantity change when the scale changes? Are the exponents physically sensible?
  7. Check the regime. Which dimensionless group is large or small, and does the law change when it crosses a threshold?

4. Demonstrations

Demonstration 27.1 — The period of a pendulum

Question. A pendulum of length LL, bob mass mm, in gravity gg, has period TT. What is the form of TT?

Variables and dimensions. T[T]T\,[T], L[L]L\,[L], g[LT−2]g\,[L T^{-2}], m[M]m\,[M]. Four variables, three independent dimensions (M,L,TM,L,T), so 4−3=14-3 = 1 dimensionless group.

Find the group. Seek exponents a,b,c,da,b,c,d with TaLbgcmd dimensionless⟹[T]a[L]b[LT−2]c[M]d=1.T^a L^b g^c m^d \text{ dimensionless} \implies [T]^{a}\,[L]^{b}\,[LT^{-2}]^{c}\,[M]^{d} = 1. Matching: MM: d=0d = 0. TT: a−2c=0a - 2c = 0. LL: b+c=0b + c = 0. Take c=1c = 1: then a=2a = 2, b=−1b = -1, d=0d = 0. So the group is Π=T2gL.\Pi = \frac{T^2 g}{L}.

Conclusion. Π=Φ(no other groups)=constant\Pi = \Phi(\text{no other groups}) = \text{constant}, so T=CLg.T = C\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}.

What to notice. Three things were obtained without solving any differential equation:

  1. The mass does not appear. The exponent d=0d=0 is forced, because mm is the only variable carrying mass and the group must be dimensionless. This is a real physical prediction, and it is correct (to the extent that the pendulum is ideal).
  2. The scaling T∝LT \propto \sqrt L is forced.
  3. The constant CC is not determined. For small oscillations, C=2πC = 2\pi, but that requires the small-angle approximation and the ODE. Dimensional analysis gives the form; the dynamics gives the constant.

This is the characteristic yield of the method: the exponents are free, the constant is not.

Demonstration 27.2 — The square–cube law

Question. How does the strength of a structure scale with its size?

Analysis. Strength is proportional to the cross-sectional area of the load-bearing material: S∝L2S \propto L^2. Weight is proportional to volume: W∝L3W \propto L^3. Hence the strength-to-weight ratio scales as SW∝L2L3=1L.\frac{S}{W} \propto \frac{L^2}{L^3} = \frac{1}{L}.

Consequence. A structure scaled up by a factor of 1010 has 1/101/10 the relative strength. This is why large animals have disproportionately thick bones (Galileo's observation), why insects can lift many times their body weight while elephants cannot, and why skyscrapers cannot simply be scaled-up houses.

Refinement. If the bone must support a weight that also scales as L3L^3, and the bone's strength scales as its cross-section d2d^2 where dd is the bone diameter, then d2∝L3d^2 \propto L^3, so d∝L3/2d \propto L^{3/2}. This is the 3/2 scaling law for bone dimensions, and it is observed across species.

What to notice. The scaling law is a constraint, not a prediction of the exact shape. It says: any structure that supports its own weight and is scaled up must violate geometric similarity. The exponent 3/23/2 came from matching two scaling relations, not from any detailed mechanics. Scaling arguments tell you what cannot be scaled, which is often more valuable than telling you what can.

Demonstration 27.3 — Kepler's third law from dimensions alone

Question. A planet orbits a star of mass MM at radius aa. How does the period TT depend on aa and MM?

Variables. T[T]T\,[T], a[L]a\,[L], M[M]M\,[M], G[L3M−1T−2]G\,[L^3 M^{-1} T^{-2}]. Four variables, three dimensions, so one group.

Find it. Seek TpaqMrGsT^p a^q M^r G^s dimensionless:

  • MM: r−s=0⟹r=sr - s = 0 \implies r = s.
  • TT: p−2s=0⟹p=2sp - 2s = 0 \implies p = 2s.
  • LL: q+3s=0⟹q=−3sq + 3s = 0 \implies q = -3s.

Take s=1s = 1: the group is T2GMa3\dfrac{T^2 G M}{a^3}. Setting it equal to a constant gives T2=4π2GMa3⟹T∝a3/2.T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\,a^3 \implies T \propto a^{3/2}.

What to notice. The 3/23/2 exponent is forced by dimensions; the constant 4π24\pi^2 is not (it comes from the circular-orbit solution). The mass dependence T∝M−1/2T \propto M^{-1/2} is also forced, and it is the reason a satellite's period depends on the central mass but not on its own mass.

This is one of the most striking successes of dimensional analysis: a law that Newton derived from a force law and Kepler inferred from data is also implied by the dimensions of GG alone. That is strong evidence that the law is over-determined — many independent constraints point to the same form.

Demonstration 27.4 — Where dimensional analysis stops

Question. What is the period of a pendulum at large amplitude?

Failure of the naive analysis. The group Π=T2g/L\Pi = T^2g/L is still the only dimensionless combination of T,L,g,mT, L, g, m. But the amplitude θ0\theta_0 is also dimensionless, so it must be included: Π=Φ(θ0)\Pi = \Phi(\theta_0). Dimensional analysis gives T=LgΦ(θ0),T = \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\,\Phi(\theta_0), with Φ\Phi an unknown function.

What is missing. The function Φ\Phi requires the equation of motion, which gives T=2πLg(1+θ0216+O(θ04)).T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\left(1 + \frac{\theta_0^2}{16} + O(\theta_0^4)\right). So the period increases with amplitude — a fact that dimensional analysis cannot see, because it cannot determine Φ\Phi.

What to notice. The failure is precise and instructive: dimensional analysis found the correct scaling and identified the correct dimensionless argument, but could not determine the function. This is the general situation. Dimensional analysis reduces the problem; it does not solve it. Knowing exactly what it leaves undetermined is what lets you combine it with a theory, an experiment, or an asymptotic expansion (→ Ch. 22).

6. Limitations

The variable list is the weak point. Omit a relevant variable and the analysis is wrong but dimensionally consistent. Include an irrelevant one and you get a spurious group. Physical judgment, not mathematics, determines the list.

Dimensionless constants are invisible. The method cannot produce 2π2\pi, 1/21/2, or any pure number. If the answer requires a constant, dimensional analysis will not give it.

Regimes are invisible. The same dimensionless group can be small or large, and the law can change qualitatively between regimes (laminar/turbulent, elastic/plastic, subsonic/supersonic). The method gives the group; the value of the group determines the regime.

Scale invariance can fail. If there is an intrinsic length (an atomic scale, a correlation length), the scaling law has corrections, and pure power-law scaling is only asymptotic. Critical phenomena are the classic case: scaling holds only near the critical point.

Not all laws are monomials. The Buckingham theorem gives a relation among groups, but if the relation is not a monomial (e.g. an exponential), dimensional analysis alone cannot find it.

7. Applications

  • Engineering. Every physical model is dimensionally checked; a dimensional error is the most common and most easily caught modeling mistake.
  • Fluid dynamics. The Reynolds, Froude, and Mach numbers determine the regime; wind-tunnel testing relies on matching dimensionless groups between model and prototype.
  • Biology. Allometric scaling (metabolic rate ∝M3/4\propto M^{3/4}, lifespan ∝M1/4\propto M^{1/4}) is an empirical scaling law whose explanation is an active research area.
  • Climate and geophysics. Scaling laws for boundary layers, convection, and turbulence organize enormous parameter spaces.
  • Finance. Scaling in market microstructure (impact ∝\propto volume1/2^{1/2}) and in firm size distributions; dimensional analysis is less applicable because the "dimensions" are economic, not physical, but the scaling logic transfers.
  • Numerical methods. Non-dimensionalization is the first step in making a simulation well-conditioned; it reveals which parameters are actually independent (→ Ch. 28).

8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

  1. List variables with dimensions, compute the rank, and form the dimensionless groups.
  2. Derive a scaling law and state what it does and does not determine.
  3. Use a scaling argument to show that a design cannot be geometrically scaled.
  4. Identify the dimensionless group that controls a regime change.
  5. Explain why a dimensional analysis can be dimensionally correct and physically wrong (wrong variable list).
  6. Combine dimensional analysis with an asymptotic or theoretical argument to determine the free function.

Diagnostic question. "What are the units, and what does homogeneity force?" A learner who can go from a variable list to a scaling law, and then say what is left undetermined, has the method.

  • Probe misconceptions: believing dimensional analysis determines constants; forgetting dimensionless arguments (angles, ratios); assuming power-law scaling always holds; ignoring regime changes.
  • Transfer distance: high, but constrained to settings with genuine dimensions. Use physics, engineering, and biology; for pure mathematics, use scaling of algorithms or geometric similarity.
