1. Core Idea

Experimental mathematics is the systematic use of computation and structured exploration to discover, test, and refine mathematical claims. It is a laboratory discipline with its own methodology:

  1. Design the experiment. Which parameter varies? What is measured? What would confirm or refute the hypothesis?
  2. Enumerate systematically. Not the cases that are convenient — the cases that span the space.
  3. Tabulate and transform. Differences, ratios, logs, residues. The right transform reveals the structure.
  4. Conjecture and test. Propose the pattern, then test it on the next case, not the cases you used.
  5. Record everything, including failures. The negative results are the most informative.

The output of an experiment is not a proof. It is a conjecture with evidence, and the evidence must be strong enough to justify the effort of proving it.

Principle signature. You have a computable family, a parameter to vary, and no formula. Or you have a formula and want to know whether it is sharp, general, or optimal.

2. First Principles

Finite differences reveal polynomial structure. If ana_n is a polynomial of degree dd in nn, then the dd-th differences are constant. Tabulating successive differences is the standard way to guess a polynomial, and the degree of the difference table is the degree of the polynomial.

Ratios reveal exponential and factorial structure. If an+1/ana_{n+1}/a_n tends to a constant, the sequence is asymptotically geometric; if it grows linearly, there is a factorial factor. Logs convert multiplicative structure to additive structure (→ Ch. 22).

Residues reveal arithmetic structure. Reducing a sequence modulo small primes reveals periodicity and divisibility patterns that are invisible in the raw values. Most number-theoretic conjectures are found this way.

Search is only as good as the search space. Enumerating n=1,…,20n = 1,\dots,20 tests a tiny corner. A good experiment varies the structure (different graphs, different primes, different dimensions), not just the index.

Confirmation bias is the main hazard. You will notice the patterns you are looking for. The defense is to state the conjecture before testing it on new cases, and to test on cases chosen to break it.

3. Reasoning Process

  1. Define the family and the parameter. What varies? What is fixed?
  2. Compute small cases by hand to check the computation itself. (An experiment on a buggy program is an experiment on the program.)
  3. Automate the enumeration over a structured range.
  4. Transform the data. Differences, ratios, logs, residues, generating function coefficients.
  5. State the conjecture, with its domain and its sharpness claim.
  6. Test on held-out cases, especially adversarial ones.
  7. Look for the mechanism — a counting argument, a recurrence, a bijection. Without a mechanism, the conjecture is a coincidence (→ Ch. 31).
  8. Record the failures, and what they ruled out.

4. Demonstrations

Demonstration 33.1 — Discovering an identity by tabulation

Experiment. Compute Sn=∑k=1nk⋅k!S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} k\cdot k! for small nn:

nn SnS_n Sn+1S_n + 1
1 11 2=2!2 = 2!
2 1+4=51 + 4 = 5 6=3!6 = 3!
3 5+18=235 + 18 = 23 24=4!24 = 4!
4 23+96=11923 + 96 = 119 120=5!120 = 5!

Conjecture. Sn=(n+1)!−1S_n = (n+1)! - 1.

Mechanism (and proof). The summand telescopes: k⋅k!=((k+1)−1)k!=(k+1)!−k!.k\cdot k! = \big((k+1)-1\big)k! = (k+1)! - k!. Summing from k=1k=1 to nn: ∑k=1n((k+1)!−k!)=(n+1)!−1!.◼\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big((k+1)! - k!\big) = (n+1)! - 1!. \qquad \blacksquare

What to notice. The table suggested the formula; the mechanism — telescoping — proved it and explained it. The mechanism was found by asking "why would k⋅k!k\cdot k! telescope?", and the answer is that k=(k+1)−1k = (k+1)-1 is exactly the difference that produces consecutive factorials.

The general lesson. When a table suggests a closed form, look for a telescoping structure first. It is the most common mechanism behind sums of the form ∑f(k)\sum f(k) with a factorial or binomial flavor, and it is the reason generating functions and finite differences are related (→ Ch. 15).

Demonstration 33.2 — Discovering a counterexample by search

Conjecture (false). 2n−12^n - 1 is prime for all n≥2n \ge 2.

Search. Compute 2n−12^n - 1 and test primality for small nn:

nn 2n−12^n-1 Prime?
2 3 yes
3 7 yes
4 15 no (3⋅53\cdot5)
5 31 yes
6 63 no
7 127 yes
8 255 no
9 511 no (7⋅737\cdot73)
10 1023 no
11 2047 no (23⋅8923\cdot89)

Observation. For composite nn, 2n−12^n-1 factors (since 2ab−12^{ab}-1 is divisible by 2a−12^a-1). So the interesting cases are prime nn. But n=11n=11 is prime and 211−1=2047=23⋅892^{11}-1 = 2047 = 23\cdot89 is composite.

Repair. The correct conjecture is about Mersenne primes: 2p−12^p - 1 is prime for some primes pp (2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, ...), not all. The search found the first failure at p=11p=11, which is close enough to be found by hand and far enough to be missed by casual checking.

What to notice. The search was structured: the parity/compositeness observation reduced the search space to prime nn, and the failure appeared immediately. Good experiments reduce the search space by structure before enumerating. Brute force over all nn would have found the same counterexample at n=4n=4 (a composite case, less informative); the structural reduction found the interesting one.

Demonstration 33.3 — Finite differences and the polynomial guess

Experiment. Count the maximum number of regions into which nn lines in general position divide the plane:

nn 0 1 2 3 4 5
RnR_n 1 2 4 7 11 16

First differences: 1,2,3,4,51, 2, 3, 4, 5. Second differences: 1,1,1,11, 1, 1, 1 — constant.

Conjecture. RnR_n is a quadratic polynomial in nn. With second difference 11, the leading coefficient is 1/21/2. Fitting Rn=12n2+bn+cR_n = \frac12 n^2 + bn + c to R0=1R_0 = 1, R1=2R_1 = 2 gives c=1c = 1 and 12+b+1=2\frac12 + b + 1 = 2, so b=12b = \frac12. Hence Rn=n2+n+22=n(n+1)2+1.R_n = \frac{n^2+n+2}{2} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} + 1. Check n=2n=2: 3+1=43+1 = 4 ✓; n=5n=5: 15+1=1615+1 = 16 ✓.

Mechanism. Adding the nn-th line: it crosses the previous n−1n-1 lines at n−1n-1 points, dividing it into nn segments, each of which splits an existing region. So Rn=Rn−1+nR_n = R_{n-1} + n, with R0=1R_0 = 1. Summing gives Rn=1+∑k=1nk=1+n(n+1)/2R_n = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^n k = 1 + n(n+1)/2. ◼\blacksquare

What to notice. The finite-difference table found the degree and the leading coefficient, which is enough to fit the rest. The recurrence found the mechanism, which gives the proof and generalizes (to hyperplanes, to circles, to other arrangements).

The division of labor is the point: the table is fast and unreliable; the mechanism is slow and certain. Use the table to guess, the mechanism to prove, and always do both.

Demonstration 33.4 — The experiment that lies

Cautionary case. The Pólya conjecture (1919): for every n≥2n \ge 2, at least half of the integers 2,…,n2,\dots,n have an odd number of prime factors (counted with multiplicity). It was verified for all nn up to hundreds of millions, and it is false: a counterexample was found (by Haselgrove, 1958) near 9×1089\times10^8.

Why the experiment was misleading. The conjecture is about a sign change in a sum that is dominated by a slowly varying main term; the sign change occurs only after the fluctuations accumulate past the main term. The first counterexample is far beyond any feasible hand computation, and even the early computer searches missed it.

What to notice. The evidence was overwhelming by the standards of any practical experiment, and the conjecture was still false. Finite verification cannot establish a universal statement, no matter how many cases (→ Ch. 4). This is not a reason to stop experimenting; it is a reason to treat experiments as evidence about mechanism, not as proof. The experiment's real value was in suggesting the mechanism (the distribution of ω(n)\omega(n)), which is what eventually led to the proof of the related theorems.

6. Limitations

Experiments cannot prove universals. No finite number of cases establishes "for all nn". This is not a technicality; it is the definition of a universal quantifier.

The search space is usually wrong. Enumerating indices tests a thin slice; the structure that matters may be in a different dimension (larger primes, different graphs, higher dimension).

Computational bugs produce mathematical "discoveries". An experiment on a buggy program discovers a property of the program. Verification against hand-computed cases is mandatory.

Patterns can be coincidences. The Moser circle problem (→ Ch. 31) is the canonical example: five cases agreeing is not evidence of a mechanism.

Recording is undervalued. The negative results — the cases that failed, the patterns that broke — are the most informative and the most often lost. Keep a log.

7. Applications

  • Mathematics. The discovery of the Rogers–Ramanujan identities, the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, and countless combinatorial identities came from systematic computation.
  • Number theory. Primality patterns, class numbers, and LL-function zeros are explored computationally before being proved.
  • Combinatorics. Enumerating small cases is how most conjectures about graphs, permutations, and partitions are born.
  • Physics. Experimental mathematics overlaps with numerical simulation; the difference is that the target is a theorem, not a measurement.
  • Machine learning. Automated conjecture generation (pattern finding in sequences, symbolic regression) is an active research area; the methodology is the one in this chapter, automated.
  • Engineering. Design-space exploration is structured experimentation; the finite-difference and scaling techniques apply to performance data.

8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

  1. Design an experiment that varies structure, not just index.
  2. Use finite differences, ratios, logs, and residues to transform data into a recognizable form.
  3. State a conjecture from data, with a mechanism, before testing it on new cases.
  4. Test adversarially on held-out and boundary cases.
  5. Recognize when a pattern is a coincidence and when it has a mechanism.
  6. Keep a record of negative results and use them to constrain the next experiment.

Diagnostic question. "You have a table of values. What do you do next?" A learner who transforms the data and looks for a mechanism has the method. A learner who stares at the numbers does not.

AI Tutor Brief

  • Generate: (i) sequences to be identified by differences/ratios; (ii) families to be enumerated with a structural reduction; (iii) patterns with a hidden failure just outside the tested range; (iv) "design the experiment" tasks where the learner must choose what to vary.
  • Probe misconceptions: "verified for many cases, therefore true"; brute force over indices as a substitute for structural variation; ignoring the possibility of a computational bug.
  • Transfer distance: high. Use sequences, graphs, and geometric configurations.
  • Do not accept a conjecture from data without a mechanism and a held-out test.
