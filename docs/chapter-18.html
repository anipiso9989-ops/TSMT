Chapter 18 / Part III

Generalize Without Breaking the Argument

On this page

  • Start here

  • Try it

Start here

A 3×53\times5 rectangular grid has 15 cells. The same argument gives abab cells for any grid with aa rows and bb columns. Generalizing means finding a family of problems that share an argument.

Worked example

With three positive numbers, each at most 5, the sum is at most 15. For nn numbers, each at most MM, the sum is at most nMnM. Add the nn individual inequalities. The reasoning never used positivity, so the bound also holds for negative numbers and negative MM. It does use the upper bound on every term. Knowing that only the first term is at most MM is insufficient.

To generalize a result, look at the steps of its proof. Replace the specific quantities by variables and keep the conditions that make each step legal.

Try it

  • Practice. A 4×44\times4 grid has 16 cells. Generalize the number of cells to a rectangular grid and then to a stack of such grids.
  • Stretch. A tournament with six players has 15 games if every pair plays once. Give a formula for nn players. Then adapt it when each pair plays exactly twice and some of the games are canceled. What extra information determines the new total?

Check your work

Practice. There are abab cells in an a×ba\times b grid and abcabc cells in a stack of cc such grids.

Stretch. Each player contributes n−1n-1 game ends, giving n(n−1)/2n(n-1)/2 games. Two games per pair give n(n−1)n(n-1) scheduled games. Subtract the number canceled. If you know only which players had cancellations, you may still need the cancellation count.
