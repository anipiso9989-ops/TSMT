See the Same Fact in Two Ways

On this page

Start here

A drawing can make a fact easy to discover. Algebra can make it easy to generalize. Comparing the two helps you see which step does the work.

Worked example

Take an n×nn\times n square of unit cells. Add a row of nn cells, a column of nn cells, and one corner cell to make the next square. The extra cells total 2n+12n+1. Algebra gives the same fact.

(n+1)2−n2=2n+1.(n+1)^2-n^2=2n+1.

The picture shows why the terms are two strips and a corner. The algebra confirms the equality and suggests a new question. If both sides grow by kk units instead of 1, how many cells are added?

Try it

  • Practice. Answer the new question in both ways. Assume n,kn,k are positive integers.
  • Stretch. Use a pairing argument and a picture of dots to explain why 1+2+⋯+n=n(n+1)/21+2+\cdots+n=n(n+1)/2. Explain where the factor of 2 appears in each version.

Check your work

Practice. The strips have nknk cells each and the new corner has k2k^2, so the total is 2nk+k22nk+k^2. Expanding (n+k)2−n2(n+k)^2-n^2 gives the same expression.

Stretch. Pair forward and reverse lists to get nn pairs of sum n+1n+1. For the picture, draw left-aligned rows of lengths 1,2,…,n1,2,\dots,n. A second copy rotated by 180∘180^\circ fills the complementary space in an n×(n+1)n\times(n+1) rectangle. In each case the construction counts two copies of the original sum.
