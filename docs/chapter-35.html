1. Core Idea

Research judgment is the ability to make good decisions under uncertainty about which problems to pursue, when to persist, when to abandon, whom to trust, and what to believe. It is not a technique; it is a set of calibrated habits.

Four components:

  1. Problem choice. The single highest-leverage decision. Working on an important problem badly beats working on an unimportant problem well.
  2. Persistence and abandonment. Both are skills, and both require a criterion. Persistence without a criterion is stubbornness; abandonment without a criterion is dilettantism.
  3. Error correction. The systematic detection of your own mistakes, before anyone else does.
  4. The long game. Research compounds. Consistent direction over years produces results that no single burst can.

Hamming's framing is the right one: the question is not "how do I solve this problem?" but "what is the most important problem I could be working on, and am I actually working on it?".

2. First Principles

Importance is a property of the problem, not of the effort. A brilliant solution to a trivial problem is a trivial contribution. Importance is judged by: does it change what others can do? does it connect things? does it survive?

Tractability is a property of the state of the field. A problem that was hopeless in 1950 may be routine in 2020 because the tools arrived. Judgment includes knowing what tools now exist.

Compounding requires direction. If each step is in a random direction, the distance from the start after nn steps grows as n\sqrt n (the drunkard's walk). If each step is in a consistent direction, it grows as nn. For n=1000n = 1000 steps, that is the difference between 3232 and 10001000 — a factor of 3131. A vision of where you are going is worth more than any single step, and the mathematics is not a metaphor; it is the actual scaling.

Errors are the default. Most research ideas are wrong. The productive response is not to avoid error but to detect it cheaply and early: state claims precisely, test them on small cases, and seek the strongest possible objection.

Studying successes beats studying failures. There are many ways to be wrong and few to be right. Learning the patterns of successful work (how the problem was chosen, how the key idea was found, how the error was caught) transfers better than a catalog of failures — though both matter.

Tolerance for ambiguity is a prerequisite. Research problems are ill-defined for a long time. People who need the problem to be clear before starting never start.

Courage is a practical requirement. The key step often looks unjustified until it works. Shannon's "average over all random codes" was a leap; so was Hamming's decision to use a metric that violated the obvious choice. Without the willingness to pursue a step that is not yet justified, the step is never taken.

3. Reasoning Process

  1. Maintain a list of important problems. Five to ten, written down, revisited quarterly. The list is the instrument; without it, attention drifts to whatever is in front of you.
  2. For each, record: why it matters, what is known, what the obstruction is, what would count as progress. A problem you cannot describe this way is not yet a research problem.
  3. Choose by importance × tractability × fit. Fit includes your tools, your taste, and your tolerance for the kind of work involved.
  4. Work in bounded sessions with a written state. What I know, what I want, what I tried, what failed. The written state is what makes resumption possible and prevents re-treading.
  5. Apply the error-correction protocol (below) to every claim before believing it.
  6. At the checkpoint, decide: progress, plateau, or dead end. Use a criterion, not a feeling.
  7. Review the list. Is the problem still important? Has the field moved? Are you working on it, or on something adjacent that is easier?

The error-correction protocol

  1. State the claim precisely (→ Ch. 2, 3). Vague claims cannot be checked.
  2. Test the smallest case and the degenerate case.
  3. Attempt the counterexample (→ Ch. 32), deliberately.
  4. Identify the weakest step and try to break it.
  5. Write the proof for an adversary — someone who wants to find the gap.
  6. Wait 24 hours and reread. Distance reveals what familiarity hides.
  7. Explain it to someone outside the field. The question you cannot answer is the gap.

4. Demonstrations

Demonstration 35.1 — The compounding argument, quantified

Model. Suppose you take n=1000n = 1000 "steps" of research effort, each of unit length.

  • Random direction: the displacement after nn steps is a random walk, with expected distance n≈32\sqrt n \approx 32.
  • Consistent direction: the displacement is n=1000n = 1000.

Interpretation. The ratio is n≈32\sqrt n \approx 32. With 10,00010{,}000 steps, the ratio is 100100. The advantage of direction grows with the length of the career.

Caveat. The model is a cartoon: research steps are not independent, and "consistent direction" is not always available (the field moves). But the qualitative conclusion is robust: a coherent research program outperforms a sequence of disconnected projects by a factor that grows with time. This is the mathematical form of Hamming's "vision" argument.

What to notice. The argument does not say "have a vision and you will succeed". It says: if you have a direction, the expected distance grows linearly rather than as a square root. The conditional is doing the work, and the condition is the hard part.

Demonstration 35.2 — Applying the important-problem test

Candidate directions for a graduate student in combinatorics.

Direction Why it matters Known Obstruction Progress would be
A. A new proof of a known theorem Low: the theorem is established Everything None — the work is expository A simpler proof, which may generalize
B. A conjecture with strong numerical evidence and no proof High: many people care The evidence, some special cases No technique controls the error term A proof in a special case, or a new technique
C. A new question at the interface of two areas Uncertain: depends on the connection Little The definitions may not be compatible A precise statement of the connection
D. A problem suggested by an application Medium: depends on the application's importance The applied literature The mathematical structure may be absent A model that is both faithful and tractable

Judgment. B is the classic high-value choice: important, with a clear obstruction, where partial progress is meaningful. A is low-risk and low-yield. C is high-variance: it could be a new field or a dead end, and it requires a period of exploration before the problem is even well-posed. D is valuable only if the application is genuinely important and the structure is real.

What to notice. The table forces the questions that most people skip: what is the obstruction? and what would count as progress? A problem without a named obstruction is not yet a research problem; it is an interest.

Demonstration 35.3 — The stuckness protocol

Situation. You have worked on a problem for three weeks with no progress.

Protocol.

  1. Write the state. What is known, what is wanted, what has been tried, what failed and why. (The "why" is the valuable part.)
  2. Ask the form question. If I had a solution, what would it look like? (→ Ch. 7, 17)
  3. Ask the assumption question. Which hypothesis have I not used? Which one am I using implicitly? (→ Ch. 7, 6)
  4. Change the problem. Generalize, specialize, invert (→ Ch. 34). Work on a variant for a week.
  5. Incubate deliberately. Set the problem aside, work on something else, and let the subconscious process it. (Hamming's observation: the temporary abandonment is a common feature of creative work.)
  6. Set a criterion for abandonment. "If I cannot name the obstruction after another two weeks, I will write up what I have learned and switch."

What to notice. Every step is a technique, not a mood. Stuckness is not a signal to try harder; it is a signal to change the representation, the problem, or the level of abstraction. The only non-technique is "persist without changing anything", and it is the one most people choose.

Demonstration 35.4 — Error correction in practice

Claim. "For every graph GG, the chromatic number is at most the maximum degree plus one."

Protocol.

  1. Precise? Yes: χ(G)≤Δ(G)+1\chi(G) \le \Delta(G) + 1.
  2. Smallest case: K1K_1: χ=1\chi=1, Δ=0\Delta=0, 1≤11 \le 1 ✓. K2K_2: χ=2\chi=2, Δ=1\Delta=1, 2≤22 \le 2 ✓.
  3. Degenerate case: the empty graph on nn vertices: χ=1\chi = 1, Δ=0\Delta = 0, 1≤11 \le 1 ✓.
  4. Counterexample hunt: the greedy argument gives Δ+1\Delta+1 (each vertex has at most Δ\Delta neighbors, so among Δ+1\Delta+1 colors one is free). The bound is attained by odd cycles: C5C_5 has χ=3\chi=3, Δ=2\Delta=2, so 3≤33 \le 3 ✓. And by KΔ+1K_{\Delta+1}: χ=Δ+1\chi = \Delta+1 ✓. So the bound is sharp.
  5. Weakest step: the greedy argument assumes a vertex ordering; it works for any ordering, so the step is robust.
  6. Adversarial reading: "at most Δ+1\Delta+1" — can it be improved to Δ\Delta? No: odd cycles and complete graphs show it cannot. The bound is sharp, and the theorem is correct as stated.

Outcome. The claim survives all six checks, and the checks have produced additional information: the bound is sharp, and the extremal graphs are odd cycles and complete graphs. Error correction is not just a filter; it is a source of understanding.

What to notice. The protocol took ten minutes and produced a stronger result than the original claim (sharpness plus the extremal examples). This is typical: the checks that catch errors are the same checks that reveal structure. A claim that survives a serious audit is a claim you understand.

6. Limitations

Judgment is not algorithmic. The criteria (importance, tractability, fit) are heuristics, and they conflict. There is no procedure that produces the right choice; there is only the disciplined application of the criteria plus experience.

Importance is often visible only in retrospect. The most important problems of a generation are frequently not recognized as such at the time. This is not a reason to ignore importance; it is a reason to be humble about your estimates of it.

The compounding argument assumes a direction that may not exist. Fields move, tools change, and a direction that was coherent at the start may be obsolete at the end. The model is a cartoon, not a guarantee.

Persistence can be a trap. The sunk-cost fallacy is real, and the people most likely to fall into it are the most capable (they have succeeded before, so they believe they will succeed again). The criterion for abandonment is the defense.

Studying successes has survivorship bias. The successful researchers who are studied are the ones who succeeded; the ones who followed the same strategy and failed are invisible (→ Ch. 29). The lessons are real but the sample is selected.

7. Applications

  • Academic research. Problem choice, grant strategy, collaboration, and the management of a multi-year program.
  • Industry research. Choosing projects with a real chance of mattering, and knowing when to kill them.
  • Engineering leadership. The same judgment applied to technical directions: which bets are worth making, and what would count as evidence that a bet is failing.
  • Any long-horizon project. The compounding argument applies to any skill or program: consistent direction dominates scattered effort.
  • Personal development. The choice of what to become good at is the same problem as the choice of what to research, with the same criteria.

8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

  1. Maintain a written list of important problems with the four fields (why, known, obstruction, progress) filled in.
  2. Apply the important-problem test to a candidate direction and articulate the obstruction.
  3. Use a written stuckness protocol with an explicit abandonment criterion.
  4. Apply the seven-step error-correction protocol to a claim of your own, and report what the audit revealed.
  5. Explain the compounding argument and its assumptions.
  6. Distinguish persistence from stubbornness, and abandonment from dilettantism, by naming the criterion in each case.

Diagnostic question. "What are you working on, why does it matter, and what is the obstruction?" A learner who can answer all three has research judgment. A learner who can answer only the first has an interest.

AI Tutor Brief

  • Generate: (i) research-direction scenarios to be evaluated with the important-problem table; (ii) stuckness scenarios requiring a protocol, not a solution; (iii) claims to be audited with the seven-step protocol; (iv) prioritization problems where the learner must choose among five projects with stated criteria.
  • Probe misconceptions: effort as a substitute for importance; persistence without a criterion; the sunk-cost fallacy; treating a selected sample of successes as evidence.
  • Transfer distance: maximal. These are life decisions, not exercises.
  • Do not grade the choice of problem. Grade the reasoning: are the criteria explicit, is the obstruction named, is the abandonment criterion stated?
