This guide draws on three books about mathematical problem solving and technical judgment. The chapter sequence, explanations, examples, and practice tasks are written for this guide.

Books behind the guide

Book Ideas that carry into these chapters
Richard W. Hamming, The Art of Doing Science and Engineering: Learning to Learn Estimation, computation as a way to gain insight, and judgment about which problems are worth pursuing. See Chs. 26, 28, 30, and 35.
Sandor Lehoczky and Richard Rusczyk, The Art of Problem Solving, Volume 1: The Basics Proof techniques, counting, working backwards, and the practice of trying to explain why a method works. See Chs. 4, 10, 12, 13, and 17.
Sandor Lehoczky and Richard Rusczyk, The Art of Problem Solving, Volume 2: And Beyond Looking for correspondences, transforming a problem, and using structure to generalize. See Chs. 14, 15, 18, 19, and 24.

How the pieces fit together

The early chapters build precise language and proof habits. The middle chapters train you to pick a method from the shape of the problem. The applied chapters ask whether the assumptions fit a real situation. The last part asks you to find your own question and decide what evidence could settle it.

Across all five parts, the same test applies. A small example should help you discover a move. A proof should explain why it works. A stretch problem should make you decide how far the idea carries.

Familiar examples

Some chapters use standard examples from mathematical problem solving. The missing-corner chessboard in Chapter 8 demonstrates an invariant. The six-person argument in Chapter 9 is the upper-bound direction of the classical Ramsey result for triples. The prime-producing polynomial in Chapter 31 is a standard example of a pattern that eventually fails. Their purpose here is to make the choice of reasoning visible.
