1. Core Idea

Logic is the study of which conclusions are forced by which premises, independent of what the premises are about. Its power is exactly its content-independence: the same rules validate an argument about primes and an argument about interest rates. Its blindness is the same fact: logic can tell you that your conclusion follows, never that your premises are worth holding, and never that your conclusion is interesting.

For the working mathematician, logic is not a subject to be studied but a hygiene discipline. It supplies four things:

  1. A canonical form for statements (connectives and quantifiers).
  2. A mechanical procedure for negation.
  3. A sharp distinction between validity (the argument's form) and truth (the premises' content).
  4. A catalog of errors that look like reasoning.

2. First Principles

Connectives. ¬\neg (not), ∧\land (and), ∨\lor (or, inclusive), ⟹\implies (implies), ⇔\iff (iff). Each is defined by its truth table. Two consequences matter constantly:

  • P⟹QP \implies Q is equivalent to ¬P∨Q\neg P \lor Q. In particular, an implication is false only when PP is true and QQ is false.
  • P⟹QP \implies Q is not the same as Q⟹PQ \implies P (converse), nor as ¬P⟹¬Q\neg P \implies \neg Q (inverse). It is equivalent to ¬Q⟹¬P\neg Q \implies \neg P (contrapositive).

Quantifiers as a game. ∀xP(x)\forall x \, P(x) means: an adversary may choose any xx in the domain, and PP must hold. ∃xP(x)\exists x \, P(x) means: you must produce a witness xx, and then PP must hold. Reading quantifiers as a game with alternating moves makes their order visceral: whoever moves later can see the earlier choices.

∀ε>0∃δ>0∀x:the δ may depend on ε but not on x.\forall \varepsilon>0\ \exists \delta>0\ \forall x: \quad \text{the } \delta \text{ may depend on } \varepsilon \text{ but not on } x. ∃δ>0∀ε>0∀x:one δ must work for all ε.\exists \delta>0\ \forall \varepsilon>0\ \forall x: \quad \text{one } \delta \text{ must work for all } \varepsilon.

These are wildly different claims, and the difference is entirely the order of the first two quantifiers.

Negation is mechanical. Push ¬\neg inward, flipping each quantifier and negating the body:

¬(∀x∃yP(x,y))≡∃x∀y¬P(x,y).\neg\big(\forall x\, \exists y\, P(x,y)\big) \equiv \exists x\, \forall y\, \neg P(x,y).

There is no need for intuition here. Negation is an algorithm, and getting it wrong is a computational error, not a conceptual one.

Vacuous truth. ∀x∈∅P(x)\forall x \in \emptyset\, P(x) is true for any PP. Likewise P⟹QP \implies Q is true whenever PP is false. This is not a convention to be tolerated; it is forced by the truth table, and it is useful: it lets theorems be stated without carving out empty cases. But it also means that a statement can be true and completely uninformative, so "true" and "substantive" are different questions.

Validity versus soundness. An argument is valid if the conclusion follows from the premises. It is sound if it is valid and the premises are true. Valid-but-unsound arguments are the standard vehicle of mathematical-looking nonsense: "All primes are odd; 22 is prime; therefore 22 is odd" is perfectly valid.

3. Reasoning Process

  1. Canonicalize. Rewrite the statement using only ¬,∧,∨,⟹,∀,∃\neg, \land, \lor, \implies, \forall, \exists, with all quantifiers at the front where possible. Remove "unless", "but", "however", "only when" by translating them explicitly.
  2. Mark the dependencies. Draw arrows from each quantified variable to the statements that may depend on it. A δ\delta that depends on xx when it should not is now visible as a stray arrow.
  3. Negate mechanically if you plan to argue by contradiction, or if you want to know what a counterexample must look like. The negation is also the specification for a counterexample search.
  4. Separate form from content. Ask: is the shape of this argument valid? Then ask: are the premises true? Do these in that order; mixing them is how people talk themselves into errors.
  5. Hunt the quantifier swap. The single most common logical error in analysis, probability, and optimization is exchanging ∀\forall and ∃\exists. Check every "there exists" for what it is allowed to depend on.
  6. Check the vacuity. Is the hypothesis satisfiable? If not, the theorem is true and empty, and you have learned nothing.

4. Demonstrations

Demonstration 3.1 — Negating a definition correctly

Definition (continuity at aa). C(a):∀ε>0∃δ>0∀x(|x−a|<δ⟹|f(x)−f(a)|<ε).C(a): \quad \forall \varepsilon>0\ \exists \delta>0\ \forall x\ \big(|x-a|<\delta \implies |f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon\big).

Negation. Apply the algorithm: ¬C(a):∃ε>0∀δ>0∃x(|x−a|<δ∧|f(x)−f(a)|≥ε).\neg C(a): \quad \exists \varepsilon>0\ \forall \delta>0\ \exists x\ \big(|x-a|<\delta \land |f(x)-f(a)|\ge \varepsilon\big).

Read as a game: there is a tolerance ε\varepsilon that you cannot meet; no matter how tightly I am required to confine xx, you can find an xx within that confinement whose value is off by at least ε\varepsilon. This is precisely the operational meaning of discontinuity, and it tells you how to prove discontinuity: exhibit the bad ε\varepsilon, then for each δ\delta exhibit the bad xx.

Worked instance. f(x)=1/xf(x) = 1/x for x≠0x \neq 0, f(0)=0f(0)=0, at a=0a=0. Take ε=1\varepsilon = 1. Given any δ>0\delta>0, choose x=min(δ/2,1/2)x = \min(\delta/2, 1/2), so |x|<δ|x|<\delta and |f(x)|=1/x≥2>1=ε|f(x)| = 1/x \ge 2 > 1 = \varepsilon. Hence ¬C(0)\neg C(0) holds: ff is discontinuous at 00. ◼\blacksquare

What to notice. The proof of discontinuity is generated by the negated formula. There was no guesswork. This is the practical payoff of mechanical negation: it converts "show that it fails" into a construction with explicit slots.

Demonstration 3.2 — The quantifier swap that changes the theorem

Statement A (continuity on a set). ∀x∈(0,1)∀ε>0∃δ>0∀y∈(0,1):|x−y|<δ⟹|f(x)−f(y)|<ε.\forall x \in (0,1)\ \forall \varepsilon>0\ \exists \delta>0\ \forall y \in (0,1): |x-y|<\delta \implies |f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon.

Statement B (uniform continuity). ∀ε>0∃δ>0∀x,y∈(0,1):|x−y|<δ⟹|f(x)−f(y)|<ε.\forall \varepsilon>0\ \exists \delta>0\ \forall x,y \in (0,1): |x-y|<\delta \implies |f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon.

In A, δ\delta may depend on xx and ε\varepsilon. In B, δ\delta may depend only on ε\varepsilon. Clearly B implies A. The converse fails.

Counterexample. f(x)=1/xf(x) = 1/x on (0,1)(0,1) satisfies A but not B.

Proof of A. Fix x∈(0,1)x \in (0,1) and ε>0\varepsilon>0. For yy with |y−x|<x/2|y-x| < x/2 we have y>x/2y > x/2, hence |f(x)−f(y)|=|y−x|xy<|y−x|x⋅(x/2)=2|y−x|x2.|f(x)-f(y)| = \frac{|y-x|}{xy} < \frac{|y-x|}{x\cdot (x/2)} = \frac{2|y-x|}{x^2}. So δ=min(x/2,εx2/2)\delta = \min(x/2,\ \varepsilon x^2/2) works. Note that δ\delta depends on xx — legitimately, because xx was chosen before δ\delta in the quantifier order.

Proof of ¬\negB. Take ε=1\varepsilon = 1. Let δ>0\delta>0 be arbitrary. Choose nn large enough that 1n<δ\frac{1}{n} < \delta and 1n−1n+1=1n(n+1)<δ\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n+1} = \frac{1}{n(n+1)} < \delta; set x=1/nx = 1/n, y=1/(n+1)y = 1/(n+1). Then |x−y|<δ|x-y|<\delta but |f(x)−f(y)|=|n−(n+1)|=1≥ε.|f(x)-f(y)| = \left|n - (n+1)\right| = 1 \ge \varepsilon. Since δ\delta was arbitrary, ¬\negB holds. ◼\blacksquare

What to notice. The same function, the same formula, and the only difference is which variable was chosen first. The failure of B is caused by the fact that as x→0x \to 0, the required δ\delta shrinks to 00 — so no single δ\delta serves all xx. When you see a quantifier-order error in your own work, look for exactly this mechanism: a quantity that blows up as a parameter varies.

Demonstration 3.3 — Converse and inverse errors

Let P(x)P(x) be "x=2x = 2" and Q(x)Q(x) be "x2=4x^2 = 4". The implication P⟹QP \implies Q is true. Its converse Q⟹PQ \implies P is false (x=−2x=-2). Its inverse ¬P⟹¬Q\neg P \implies \neg Q is false for the same reason. Its contrapositive ¬Q⟹¬P\neg Q \implies \neg P is true.

Why this matters in practice. A problem that asks you to solve x2=4x^2=4 is asking for the set defined by QQ, which properly contains the set defined by PP. If you "solve" by assuming PP, you have found one point and called it the answer. This is the mechanism behind the most common lost-marks error in algebra: performing a non-reversible operation (squaring, multiplying by an expression that might be zero, applying a non-injective function) and then failing to check.

What to notice. Every algebraic step is an implication. Steps that are equivalences preserve the solution set. Steps that are only implications enlarge it, and require a verification pass at the end. Track which is which, explicitly, while you work.

Demonstration 3.4 — Vacuous truth, used well and used badly

Used well. "Every element of the empty set is a purple elephant" is true. This lets us state "every prime p>2p>2 is odd" without a caveat, and it lets the statement "for all x∈Sx \in S, P(x)P(x)" be true even when SS is empty — which is exactly what we want when a theorem's hypothesis happens to be unsatisfiable.

Used badly. "All unicorns are blue, therefore some unicorn is blue." The premise is vacuously true; the conclusion does not follow. The error is treating ∀\forall as implying ∃\exists, which requires a nonempty domain.

What to notice. ∀xP(x)\forall x\, P(x) and ∃xP(x)\exists x\, P(x) differ by an existence assumption. In every theorem you use, ask whether the domain is guaranteed nonempty. This is precisely the gap in many false proofs about maxima and minima: "the maximum is attained at the point where..." presumes the maximum exists.

6. Limitations

Logic does not generate. It audits. A valid proof of an uninteresting theorem is still uninteresting. The creative work — choosing definitions, conjecturing the right statement, finding the key construction — is not logical inference and is not mechanizable in practice.

Formal validity can mask material failure. An argument can be impeccable in form and useless in content because a premise is false, or because a term is being used equivocally. Logic sees only the form.

Expressiveness limits. First-order logic cannot express "for every property PP" without ascending to second order, and sufficiently rich formal systems contain statements neither provable nor refutable within them. This is a real boundary on what formal derivation can deliver — but it is a boundary on formal systems, not on mathematical practice, which routinely steps outside a system to a richer one.

The map of "all errors" is not logical. Most actual mathematical mistakes are not logical fallacies. They are: using an unproved lemma, misreading a definition, generalizing from three cases, or drawing the picture wrong. Logic catches only the subset that is formally invalid.

7. Applications

  • Program correctness. Loop invariants and pre/post-conditions are quantified statements; a Hoare triple is an implication. Debugging is often the construction of the counterexample to a false triple.
  • Contract design. "For all inputs satisfying PP, the function returns a value satisfying QQ" is a specification; the negation tells you exactly what a failing test case must look like.
  • Experimental science. A hypothesis is a quantified claim; a single well-chosen counterexample refutes it, while confirmation instances never prove it. This asymmetry is the logical core of the scientific method.
  • Risk and regulation. "The system is safe" requires a quantifier: safe for all inputs, or for all inputs in a specified distribution, or with probability at least 1−α1-\alpha? These are different claims with different evidence requirements.
  • AI evaluation. "The model is correct" versus "the model is correct on the test distribution" is a quantifier distinction that determines whether a benchmark result means anything at all.

8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

  1. Translate an English mathematical statement into canonical quantifier form, and back, without loss.
  2. Negate any statement mechanically, and read the negation as an operational recipe.
  3. Identify the dependence structure of every quantified variable in a definition and say which dependencies are legitimate.
  4. Distinguish validity from soundness in a given argument, and construct a valid-but-unsound argument on demand.
  5. State precisely when vacuous truth applies, and detect when a proof has assumed existence without proving it.
  6. Given an algebraic manipulation, classify each step as an equivalence or a one-way implication, and say what verification it therefore requires.

Diagnostic question. "Negate: for every ε\varepsilon there is NN such that for all n>Nn>N, |an−L|<ε|a_n - L|<\varepsilon." A learner who produces ∃ε∀N∃n>N\exists \varepsilon\ \forall N\ \exists n>N with |an−L|≥ε|a_n-L|\ge\varepsilon has the skill. A learner who produces "for every ε\varepsilon there is NN such that for all n>Nn>N, |an−L|≥ε|a_n-L|\ge\varepsilon" has negated the wrong part.

AI Tutor Brief

  • Generate: (i) statements to negate, escalating from single quantifiers to four alternating ones; (ii) quantifier-order pairs where one is true and the other false, with a request to prove both; (iii) arguments containing a converse/inverse error or a vacuous-existence error, to be diagnosed; (iv) "which step is not reversible?" audits of algebraic solutions.
  • Probe misconceptions: swapping ∀∃\forall\exists; negating the consequent instead of the whole implication; treating ∀\forall as implying ∃\exists; believing a true implication licenses its converse.
  • Transfer distance: medium-high. Logic transfers perfectly in form, so the difficulty must come from content: use unfamiliar definitions from analysis, probability, or combinatorics.
  • Do not accept prose negations. Require symbols, in canonical order, with the dependency structure visible.
