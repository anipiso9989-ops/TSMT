1. Core Idea

Probability enters mathematics in three distinct roles, and conflating them is the source of most confusion:

  1. Probability as a model of uncertainty. The world is random (or our knowledge is incomplete), and probability quantifies it.
  2. Probability as a counting device. A uniform distribution converts counting into averaging, and averaging often proves existence: if the average is XX, some object is at least XX.
  3. Probability as a proof technique. The probabilistic method proves that an object with a property exists by showing that a random object has the property with positive probability.

The third role is the one that most surprises students: randomness is a tool for proving deterministic theorems. No randomness is assumed in the conclusion; the random construction is only a device for showing that the set of good objects is nonempty.

Principle signature. The problem asks to prove existence of an object with many constraints, where direct construction is hard; or the problem involves averages, expected values, or "at least one"; or the problem is about a process with many interacting parts.

2. First Principles

Linearity of expectation is unconditional. For any random variables X1,…,XnX_1,\dots,X_n, regardless of dependence, 𝔼[∑iXi]=∑i𝔼[Xi].\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_i X_i\right] = \sum_i \mathbb{E}[X_i]. This is the single most useful fact in the subject, because it lets you decompose a complicated expectation into simple indicators. No independence is required — this is what makes it so powerful.

The averaging (existence) principle. If 𝔼[X]≥μ\mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mu, then some outcome has X≥μX \ge \mu. Contrapositive: if every outcome had X<μX < \mu, the expectation would be <μ< \mu. This is how expectation proves existence.

The probabilistic method. To show an object with property PP exists: choose a random object from a distribution, and show Pr[P]>0\Pr[P] > 0. Equivalently, show that the expected number of "bad events" is less than 11, so some outcome has no bad event. The bound obtained is usually non-constructive but often nearly tight.

Conditional probability and Bayes. Pr[A∣B]=Pr[A∩B]/Pr[B]\Pr[A \mid B] = \Pr[A\cap B]/\Pr[B], and Pr[A∣B]=Pr[B∣A]Pr[A]Pr[B].\Pr[A\mid B] = \frac{\Pr[B\mid A]\Pr[A]}{\Pr[B]}. The base rate Pr[A]\Pr[A] is usually the dominant term, and ignoring it is the most common error in applied probability.

Variance and concentration. Expectation tells you the center; variance tells you the spread. A bound on the mean says nothing about typical values. Concentration inequalities (Markov, Chebyshev, Chernoff) quantify how far a random variable can stray, and they are what turn "the average is good" into "most objects are good".

3. Reasoning Process

  1. Define the random experiment. What is random, and with what distribution? Be explicit — the choice of distribution is a modeling decision.
  2. Define indicator variables for the events you care about. Decompose the quantity of interest as a sum of indicators.
  3. Apply linearity to compute the expectation without worrying about dependence.
  4. Use the averaging principle to conclude existence.
  5. For the probabilistic method, bound the expected number of bad events and show it is <1<1.
  6. Check the conclusion is deterministic. The theorem must not mention probability. If it does, you have proved a statement about the model, not about the objects.
  7. For conditional statements, identify the base rate before computing anything.

4. Demonstrations

Demonstration 21.1 — Linearity without independence

Claim. The expected number of fixed points of a uniformly random permutation of {1,…,n}\{1,\dots,n\} is 11.

Proof. Let XiX_i be the indicator of the event σ(i)=i\sigma(i) = i. Then X=∑iXiX = \sum_i X_i is the number of fixed points. For each ii, Pr[σ(i)=i]=1/n\Pr[\sigma(i)=i] = 1/n (by symmetry: ii is equally likely to be sent to any of the nn positions). Hence 𝔼[X]=∑i=1n𝔼[Xi]=∑i=1n1n=1.◼\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_i] = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac1n = 1. \qquad \blacksquare

What to notice. The indicators XiX_i are not independent — if σ(1)=1\sigma(1)=1 then σ(2)=2\sigma(2)=2 is slightly less likely. Linearity does not care. This is the whole reason the technique works: dependence affects variance, not the mean.

The limitation, immediately. 𝔼[X]=1\mathbb{E}[X]=1 does not mean a typical permutation has one fixed point. In fact, the probability of having no fixed points tends to 1/e≈0.3681/e \approx 0.368, and the distribution is roughly Poisson with mean 11. The expectation is a summary, not a description. Never infer the typical value from the mean (→ Ch. 29).

Demonstration 21.2 — The probabilistic method

Claim. R(4,4)≥7R(4,4) \ge 7: there is a red/blue coloring of the edges of K6K_6 with no monochromatic K4K_4.

Proof. Color each edge of K6K_6 red or blue independently with probability 1/21/2. For a fixed set SS of 44 vertices, the probability that all (42)=6\binom{4}{2}=6 edges inside SS have the same color is 2⋅2−6=2−5=1/322\cdot 2^{-6} = 2^{-5} = 1/32.

There are (64)=15\binom{6}{4} = 15 such 44-sets. Let XX be the number of monochromatic K4K_4's. By linearity, 𝔼[X]=15⋅132=1532<1.\mathbb{E}[X] = 15\cdot\frac{1}{32} = \frac{15}{32} < 1. Since XX is a non-negative integer and its expectation is less than 11, some coloring has X=0X = 0 — that is, no monochromatic K4K_4. Hence such a coloring exists, and R(4,4)≥7R(4,4) \ge 7. ◼\blacksquare

What to notice. The argument proves existence without producing the coloring. It is also quantitative: a random coloring has positive probability of being good, and the expected number of bad configurations is 15/32<115/32 < 1, which is exactly the condition that forces X=0X = 0 for some coloring. The bound is far from tight — the true value is R(4,4)=18R(4,4) = 18 — but it took three lines, and for larger parameters the probabilistic method is essentially the only known way to obtain lower bounds of this kind.

The technique in general form: define a random object, count the expected number of bad events, and if the expectation is <1<1, a good object exists. This is the first moment method, and it is the entry point to the whole probabilistic-combinatorics literature.

Demonstration 21.3 — Bayes and the base rate

Problem. A disease affects 11 in 10,00010{,}000 people. A test is 99%99\% sensitive (positive given disease) and 99%99\% specific (negative given no disease). A random person tests positive. What is the probability they have the disease?

Computation. Let DD be "has disease" and ++ be "tests positive". We know Pr[D]=10−4\Pr[D] = 10^{-4}, Pr[+|D]=0.99\Pr[+|D] = 0.99, Pr[+|¬D]=0.01\Pr[+|\neg D] = 0.01. Then Pr[D∣+]=Pr[+|D]Pr[D]Pr[+|D]Pr[D]+Pr[+|¬D]Pr[¬D]=0.99⋅10−40.99⋅10−4+0.01⋅0.9999.\Pr[D\mid +] = \frac{\Pr[+|D]\Pr[D]}{\Pr[+|D]\Pr[D] + \Pr[+|\neg D]\Pr[\neg D]} = \frac{0.99\cdot 10^{-4}}{0.99\cdot10^{-4} + 0.01\cdot 0.9999}. Numerically: numerator ≈9.9×10−5\approx 9.9\times10^{-5}; denominator ≈9.9×10−5+9.999×10−3≈1.0098×10−2\approx 9.9\times10^{-5} + 9.999\times10^{-3} \approx 1.0098\times10^{-2}. So Pr[D∣+]≈9.9×10−51.0098×10−2≈0.0098≈1%.\Pr[D\mid +] \approx \frac{9.9\times10^{-5}}{1.0098\times10^{-2}} \approx 0.0098 \approx 1\%.

What to notice. A "99% accurate" test on a rare condition yields a positive result that is wrong 99%99\% of the time. The reason is arithmetic, not paradox: the false positives (1%1\% of 9,9999{,}999 healthy people ≈100\approx 100 people) vastly outnumber the true positives (99%99\% of 11 sick person ≈1\approx 1 person). The base rate dominates. Any claim of the form "the test is 99% accurate" is uninformative without the prevalence.

This is the single most practically important calculation in the chapter, and it generalizes: whenever you evaluate evidence, compare the likelihood ratio to the prior odds, not the raw accuracy.

Demonstration 21.4 — Expectation versus typical value

Claim. In a random permutation of nn letters placed randomly into nn envelopes, the expected number of correctly placed letters is 11, but the probability that no letter is correctly placed tends to 1/e1/e.

Sketch. The expectation is 11 by Demonstration 21.1. For the probability of no fixed point (a derangement), inclusion–exclusion gives Pr[no fixed point]=∑k=0n(−1)kk!→n→∞e−1≈0.368.\Pr[\text{no fixed point}] = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!} \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} e^{-1} \approx 0.368.

What to notice. The mean is 11 and the most likely value is 00. The distribution is spread across 0,1,2,…0,1,2,\dots with a long tail. A mean of 11 is compatible with a typical value of 00, and confusing the two is the source of a great deal of bad reasoning in finance, medicine, and public policy (→ Ch. 29).

6. Limitations

Expectation is not typical. The mean can be attained with probability zero, and the typical value can be far from the mean (Demonstration 21.4). Always ask for the distribution, or at least the variance.

The probabilistic method is non-constructive. It proves existence without producing the object. For algorithmic purposes you need derandomization or an explicit construction.

Independence is usually assumed, rarely true. Linearity does not need it, but variance computations, concentration bounds, and most "multiply the probabilities" arguments do. Assuming independence when it fails is the most common technical error in applied probability.

Base rates are ignored. Bayesian calculations are only as good as the prior; a wrong prior produces a confidently wrong posterior. This is not a flaw of the mathematics but of the modeling.

Rare events are underestimated. Models with thin tails (Gaussian) assign vanishing probability to events that occur regularly in practice. The 2008 crisis is the canonical example: the models assumed independence and thin tails, and both assumptions failed simultaneously.

7. Applications

  • Finance. Option pricing is expectation under a risk-neutral measure; risk management is the study of the tails; the failure modes are dependence and fat tails.
  • Medicine. Screening, diagnostic tests, and clinical trials are Bayesian inference with base rates; the base-rate fallacy is a documented cause of misdiagnosis.
  • Machine learning. Generalization bounds are probabilistic statements about the gap between training and test error; they depend on assumptions (i.i.d., bounded loss) that are often violated.
  • Reliability engineering. Failure rates, redundancy, and mean time to failure are probabilistic models; the assumption of independence between components is the usual point of failure.
  • Algorithm design. Randomized algorithms (quicksort, hashing, Monte Carlo) trade determinism for simplicity and speed; the analysis is concentration of measure.
  • Statistical mechanics. Macroscopic quantities are expectations over microstates; the concentration of measure is why thermodynamics is deterministic despite microscopic randomness.

8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

  1. Set up a random experiment and compute an expectation by decomposing into indicators.
  2. Use linearity without assuming independence, and explain why it is valid.
  3. Prove existence by the first-moment method, and state clearly that the proof is non-constructive.
  4. Compute a posterior probability and explain the role of the base rate.
  5. Distinguish the mean from the typical value, and give an example where they differ.
  6. Identify the independence or distributional assumptions in a probabilistic argument and say what happens if they fail.

Diagnostic question. "The expected value is 11. What does that tell you about a typical case?" A learner who says "nothing without the distribution" has the concept.
