# Part V — Independent Mathematical Research

> *In Parts I–IV the problem was given. Here it is not. The final five chapters train the abilities that make a mathematician independent: forming conjectures, breaking them, experimenting, inventing problems, and judging which problems deserve a life.*

**The shift in standards**

Up to here, correctness was the standard. From here, the standards are:

- **Productive wrongness.** A precise conjecture that turns out false is more valuable than a vague claim that turns out true, because the failure localizes the mechanism.
- **Falsifiability.** Every claim must come with a description of what would refute it.
- **Taste.** Among infinitely many true statements, only a few are worth proving. Taste is the ability to tell which, and it is trainable — not by rule, but by exposure to many examples of both kinds.

# Chapter 31 — Conjecture Formation

## 1. Core Idea

A **conjecture** is a precisely stated mathematical claim that you believe but cannot yet prove. Conjecture formation is the disciplined transition from observation to claim:

$$\text{observe} \to \text{notice a pattern} \to \text{find a mechanism} \to \text{state precisely} \to \text{test} \to \text{refine}$$

The critical step is **mechanism**. A pattern without a mechanism is a coincidence with a good publicist. A pattern with a mechanism is a theorem waiting for a proof — and the mechanism usually tells you the proof strategy.

The second critical step is **precision**. "The primes are randomly distributed" is not a conjecture; it cannot be refuted, so it cannot be tested, so it cannot be research. "The number of primes in $[x, x+h]$ is asymptotic to $h/\ln x$ when $h$ grows faster than $x^{1/2+\varepsilon}$" is a conjecture: it is quantitative, falsifiable, and connected to a mechanism.

**Principle signature.** You have data — numerical, combinatorial, structural — and a regularity that persists. Or you have a theorem and wonder how far it extends.

## 2. First Principles

**Plausibility heuristics.** Conjectures are generated by heuristics, and knowing the heuristics tells you how much to trust the conjecture:

| Heuristic | Form | Reliability |
|---|---|---|
| **Analogy** | "This is like that known case, so the same should hold." | Medium — depends on the strength of the structural correspondence. |
| **Simplicity** | "The simplest formula consistent with the data." | Low — simplicity is a prior, not evidence. |
| **Density/probability** | "There are so many chances for it to happen that failure is unlikely." | Medium-high for existence, low for structure. |
| **Extremal consistency** | "It holds at the extremes, so it should hold between." | Medium — fails at phase transitions. |
| **Symmetry** | "The answer must respect the symmetry of the problem." | High when the symmetry is exact, misleading when it is broken. |
| **Asymptotic matching** | "The asymptotic prediction agrees with the small cases." | Medium — small cases can be unrepresentative. |

**Degrees of belief, honestly held.** A conjecture is not a binary. Rank your confidence and record *why*: which heuristic supports it, which cases you checked, which mechanism you believe in. When it fails, the record tells you which heuristic was wrong, which is the actual learning.

**Falsifiability is the entry ticket.** A conjecture must specify what would refute it. If you cannot describe a potential counterexample, you have not stated a mathematical claim; you have stated a mood.

**Statement quality determines attackability.** Good conjectures are:
- **Quantified:** "for all $n \ge N$" with $N$ specified, or "for infinitely many $n$", not "in general".
- **Sharp:** they assert the best possible constant or the exact threshold, not a loose bound.
- **Local:** they isolate one mechanism, so a failure localizes the error.
- **Connected:** they imply, or are implied by, other statements — so progress on one is progress on many.

## 3. Reasoning Process

1. **Collect data systematically.** Small cases, extremal cases, random cases, asymptotic cases. Not the cases that are easy — the cases that are *informative*.
2. **Look for invariants in the data.** What stays the same as the parameter changes? Ratios, differences, residues, signs, growth rates.
3. **Propose a mechanism.** Why *would* the pattern hold? A counting argument, a symmetry, an extremal configuration, a conservation law.
4. **State the conjecture precisely.** Quantifiers, domain, sharpness, and the conditions under which it should fail.
5. **Test adversarially.** Look for the smallest case that could break it, the boundary of the domain, the degenerate configuration.
6. **Refine.** Either strengthen (the mechanism suggests more) or weaken (the failures suggest a hypothesis you missed).
7. **Record.** The conjecture, the evidence, the mechanism, the confidence, and the falsification condition.

## 4. Demonstrations

### Demonstration 31.1 — A pattern that breaks, and what the break teaches

*Problem.* Place $n$ points on a circle, draw all chords, and assume no three chords are concurrent inside the circle. Into how many regions is the disk divided?

*Data.*

| $n$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Regions | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | **31** |

*The tempting conjecture.* "The number of regions is $2^{n-1}$."

*The failure.* At $n = 6$ the count is $31$, not $32$. The conjecture is false, and it fails at the first case where the "no three concurrent" condition starts to matter in a non-generic way — five chords through a single interior point are excluded, and the count drops by one.

*The correct formula.* The number of regions is
$$R_n = \binom{n}{4} + \binom{n}{2} + 1.$$
Check: $n=1$: $0+0+1=1$ ✓; $n=2$: $0+1+1=2$ ✓; $n=3$: $0+3+1=4$ ✓; $n=4$: $1+6+1=8$ ✓; $n=5$: $5+10+1=16$ ✓; $n=6$: $15+15+1=31$ ✓.

*The mechanism.* Use Euler's formula. Each interior intersection point comes from a choice of $4$ points (the two chords), so $V_{\text{int}} = \binom{n}{4}$; the vertices on the circle are the $n$ points. Each chord is cut by the interior points on it, and counting edges gives $E$. Then $V - E + F = 2$ gives $R_n$. (The computation is a good exercise; the point here is that the formula has a *derivation*, not just a fit.)

**What to notice.** The $2^{n-1}$ pattern held for five consecutive cases and failed at the sixth. **Five cases is not evidence of a mechanism; it is evidence of a coincidence.** The correct formula was found by asking *why* the count should have the form it does — and the "why" (Euler's formula, with $\binom{n}{4}$ interior vertices) immediately produced the $\binom{n}{4}$ term that the power of two could not explain.

This is the central lesson of conjecture formation: **when you find a pattern, immediately ask what mechanism would produce it.** If you cannot answer, treat the pattern as a coincidence and keep looking.

### Demonstration 31.2 — Refining a conjecture: the Basel problem

*Data.* Partial sums $S_N = \sum_{k=1}^{N} 1/k^2$:

| $N$ | $S_N$ |
|---|---|
| 10 | 1.549768 |
| 100 | 1.634984 |
| 1000 | 1.643935 |
| 10000 | 1.644834 |
| 100000 | 1.644924 |

*First conjecture.* The series converges (the partial sums are increasing and appear bounded).

*Refined conjecture.* The limit is $\pi^2/6 \approx 1.644934$. The identification is not from the digits — it is from the *structure*: the problem is about squares, the sum is over all integers, and $\pi$ appears in sums over lattices. Euler's derivation used the product expansion of $\sin x$ and matched coefficients.

*What the refinement required.* Not more digits — more structure. The numerical value told Euler *what* to prove; the product formula told him *how*.

**What to notice.** The conjecture went through three stages: existence (converges), value (a specific number), and mechanism (the sine product). Each stage was a separate claim with separate evidence. **A conjecture that is only "the limit is about 1.6449" is not yet mathematics**; it becomes mathematics when it is identified with a closed form that can be proved.

The practical lesson: when refining a conjecture, ask *what would make this provable?* A closed form, a recurrence, a generating function, an identity — these are the forms in which conjectures become theorems.

### Demonstration 31.3 — A plausibility argument that is not a proof

*Conjecture (Goldbach).* Every even integer $N > 2$ is a sum of two primes.

*Plausibility heuristic.* For large $N$, the number of primes below $N$ is about $N/\ln N$. If primes behaved like a random subset of the integers with density $1/\ln N$, the number of ways to write $N = p + q$ would be about
$$\frac{N}{(\ln N)^2}.$$
For $N = 10^9$ this is about $10^9/(20.7)^2 \approx 2.3\times10^6$ representations. A failure would require *every one* of these to be absent, which is fantastically unlikely if the primes are even approximately random.

*What this does and does not establish.* It establishes that the conjecture is *morally certain* under a randomness heuristic. It does not establish the conjecture, because the primes are not random — they are deterministic, and the heuristic ignores all arithmetic structure (for instance, the parity of $p$ and $q$ is constrained: for even $N$, both must be odd except in the case $N = 4 = 2+2$).

**What to notice.** The heuristic is genuinely informative: it tells you *where to look* for a counterexample (nowhere, essentially) and *why the conjecture is hard* (the difficulty is in the arithmetic structure that the heuristic discards). It also tells you what a proof would need to do: it would need to control the arithmetic correlations that the random model ignores.

**This is the correct use of plausibility arguments: not as evidence for belief, but as a guide to mechanism.** A conjecture supported by a heuristic is a conjecture with a research program attached.

### Demonstration 31.4 — Stating a conjecture so it can be attacked

*Vague.* "The primes are distributed randomly."

*Why it is not a conjecture.* It cannot be refuted: any finite pattern of primes is consistent with *some* notion of randomness. It also cannot be proved, because it has no precise content.

*Precise (a version of the prime number theorem with an error term).* "There is a constant $c > 0$ such that for all $x \ge 2$,
$$\left|\pi(x) - \operatorname{li}(x)\right| \le c\, x \exp\left(-c\sqrt{\ln x}\right),$$
where $\pi(x)$ is the number of primes $\le x$ and $\operatorname{li}(x) = \int_2^x dt/\ln t$."

*Why it is a conjecture.* It is quantitative, it specifies the domain, it has a falsification condition (find an $x$ violating the bound), and it is connected to other statements (it implies the prime number theorem and is related to the Riemann hypothesis).

**What to notice.** The vague statement and the precise statement are "about the same thing", but only one of them can be worked on. **Precision is not pedantry; it is the difference between a research program and a sentiment.** When you find yourself believing something vague, the most valuable thing you can do is force it into a quantified statement — even if the statement is wrong, because a wrong precise statement can be refuted, and refutation is progress.

- **← Ch. 7.** Anatomy of a problem is the same skill applied to a claim: state, goal, constraints.
- **← Ch. 12, 18.** Counting and generalization generate the patterns that become conjectures.
- **← Ch. 22.** Asymptotic heuristics are the most reliable conjecture generators in number theory and combinatorics.
- **→ Ch. 32.** Every conjecture needs a boundary hunt; the counterexample is the conjecture's most informative outcome.
- **→ Ch. 33.** Experimentation is how data are generated; conjecture formation is what the data are for.
- **→ Ch. 35.** Choosing which conjecture to pursue is the central act of research judgment.

## 6. Limitations

**Heuristics can be systematically wrong.** The density heuristic fails when arithmetic correlations dominate (Goldbach's parity constraint is a trivial example; the failure of naive randomness for primes is the deep one). Every heuristic has a domain.

**Patterns in small cases are unreliable.** The Moser circle problem failed at $n=6$; the Pólya conjecture held for hundreds of millions of cases and then failed. Small cases test the statement, not the mechanism.

**Precision can be premature.** Formalizing a conjecture before you understand the mechanism can lock you into the wrong statement. Iterate: vague → precise → test → refine.

**Conjectures can be unfalsifiable in practice.** A statement about numbers too large to compute is falsifiable in principle and not in practice. This is a real limitation on the empirical method, and it is why proofs matter.

**Belief is not evidence.** The strength of your conviction is not a reason for anyone else to believe the conjecture. Record the evidence, not the feeling.

## 7. Applications

- **Mathematics.** The Riemann hypothesis, Goldbach, twin primes, and the Collatz conjecture are the canonical examples: precise, falsifiable, supported by massive evidence, and unproved.
- **Theoretical computer science.** $\mathbf{P} \ne \mathbf{NP}$ is a conjecture with enormous evidence (no known algorithm, many failed attempts) and no proof; the conjecture's precise statement is what makes the research program possible.
- **Physics.** Conjectures about the spectrum, about confinement, and about the string landscape are precise claims awaiting proof or refutation.
- **Data science.** A hypothesis is a conjecture with a statistical falsification condition; the discipline of pre-registration is the discipline of stating it before looking at the data.
- **Engineering.** A design conjecture ("this architecture will scale to $10^6$ users") is falsifiable by a load test; stating it precisely is what makes the test informative.

## 8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

1. Generate a pattern from data and immediately ask what mechanism would produce it.
2. State a conjecture with explicit quantifiers, domain, and sharpness.
3. Give a falsification condition for any conjecture you state.
4. Distinguish a plausibility heuristic from evidence, and say which heuristic you used.
5. Refine a conjecture from existence to value to mechanism.
6. Identify the case that would most likely break your conjecture, and test it.

**Diagnostic question.** "What would refute this?" A learner who cannot answer has not stated a conjecture. A learner who names the smallest likely counterexample has.

### AI Tutor Brief

- **Generate:** (i) data sequences with a tempting false pattern and a true formula; (ii) vague claims to be made precise; (iii) conjectures to be tested adversarially; (iv) plausibility heuristics to be evaluated (which are reliable here, and why?).
- **Probe misconceptions:** "it holds for 10 cases, so it's true"; simplicity as evidence; treating a heuristic as a proof; unfalsifiable statements as conjectures.
- **Transfer distance:** maximal. Every problem should require the learner to *state* something, not just solve something.
- **Do not** reward a correct conjecture without a mechanism and a falsification condition.

# Chapter 32 — Counterexamples and Boundary Hunting

## 1. Core Idea

A theorem is only as strong as its weakest hypothesis. **Boundary hunting** is the systematic search for the case where a claim fails, and it serves three purposes:

1. **Refutation.** One counterexample destroys a universal claim.
2. **Understanding.** The counterexample shows exactly what a hypothesis was doing.
3. **Sharpness.** The boundary case shows that a bound cannot be improved.

The methods are systematic, not creative:

- **Small cases.** $n = 0, 1, 2$. Empty set, singleton, trivial group.
- **Degenerate cases.** Collinear points, zero-length segments, repeated roots, disconnected graphs, the zero vector.
- **Extremal cases.** The maximum, the minimum, the boundary of the domain.
- **Hypothesis deletion.** Remove one hypothesis at a time and look for a failure.
- **Minimal counterexample.** If a counterexample exists, a minimal one exists; analyze its structure.

**Principle signature.** A claim is universal ("for all", "every", "always"), and you want to know whether it is true, how strong it is, or what its hypotheses are for.

## 2. First Principles

**Universals fail at one point.** $\forall x\, P(x)$ is refuted by a single $x$ with $\neg P(x)$. This asymmetry is the whole reason counterexamples are powerful: the cost of refutation is one object; the cost of proof is an argument.

**The failure localizes the hypothesis.** If a claim fails when hypothesis $H$ is dropped, then $H$ is load-bearing. If it survives, $H$ is redundant. This is the empirical method for finding the minimal hypothesis set (→ Ch. 18).

**Minimal counterexamples have structure.** If a counterexample exists, then by well-ordering a minimal one exists (→ Ch. 10). The minimality is an extra hypothesis, and it usually forces a contradiction. This is the engine behind infinite descent and behind many impossibility proofs.

**Sharpness is a property of bounds.** A bound $f \le M$ is sharp if there is a case with $f = M$. A bound without a sharpness example is a statement about a possibly loose constant, and it may be improvable. Sharpness examples are as important as the bound.

**Diagrams encode assumptions.** A figure drawn with a point "inside" a triangle silently assumes an ordering that may fail. This is the mechanism behind the classical false proofs in geometry (all triangles isosceles, all angles equal), and it is why a case analysis must be driven by the algebra, not the picture.

## 3. Reasoning Process

1. **List the hypotheses.** Each one is a candidate for deletion.
2. **Delete one at a time.** For each, try to construct a counterexample. If you find one, the hypothesis is necessary; if you cannot, try to prove the theorem without it.
3. **Test the degenerate cases.** The empty set, zero, one, the trivial object, the collinear configuration, the repeated root.
4. **Test the extremes.** The maximum and minimum of each parameter; the boundary of each domain.
5. **Look for the minimal counterexample** if a counterexample is believed to exist, and analyze its forced structure.
6. **Check sharpness.** Exhibit the case where the bound is attained, or prove it is not.
7. **Re-examine the diagram.** If the proof used a picture, redraw it in a different configuration and see whether the argument still applies.

## 4. Demonstrations

### Demonstration 32.1 — A plausible divisibility claim, refuted

*Claim.* If $a \mid bc$, then $a \mid b$ or $a \mid c$.

*Counterexample.* $a = 6$, $b = 2$, $c = 3$: $6 \mid 6 = 2\cdot3$, but $6 \nmid 2$ and $6 \nmid 3$.

*Diagnosis.* The claim is Euclid's lemma, which requires $a$ to be **prime** (or coprime to one of the factors). The counterexample shows exactly which hypothesis is missing: $6 = 2\cdot3$ splits the divisibility between the two factors.

*Repair.* If $p$ is prime and $p \mid bc$, then $p \mid b$ or $p \mid c$. The proof uses the fact that a prime has no nontrivial factorization — the very property the counterexample exploited.

**What to notice.** The counterexample did not just refute; it *identified the missing hypothesis* and *suggested the correct theorem*. This is the typical yield of a well-chosen counterexample: the failure mode is the hypothesis.

### Demonstration 32.2 — Minimal degree is not enough for a Hamiltonian cycle

*Claim (false).* Every connected graph with minimum degree at least $2$ has a Hamiltonian cycle.

*Counterexample.* Two triangles joined by a single edge (a "bridge"). Every vertex has degree $2$ or $3$; the graph is connected; but any cycle that visits all six vertices would have to cross the bridge twice, which is impossible in a simple cycle.

*Diagnosis.* Minimum degree controls local connectivity, not global. The bridge is a **cut edge**: removing it disconnects the graph, and a Hamiltonian cycle cannot contain a cut edge (removing one edge of a cycle leaves a path, which is connected).

*Repair.* A sufficient condition must control global connectivity: e.g. Dirac's theorem requires minimum degree $\ge n/2$, which forces enough edges to preclude bridges and much more.

**What to notice.** The counterexample is minimal in a precise sense: it is the smallest graph with a cut edge and minimum degree $2$. **When a conjecture fails, look for the smallest failure — its structure is usually the explanation.**

### Demonstration 32.3 — The diagram that assumed its conclusion

*The classical false proof.* "Every triangle is isosceles." Let $ABC$ be a triangle. Draw the angle bisector of $\angle B$ and the perpendicular bisector of $AC$; let them meet at $E$. Drop perpendiculars from $E$ to $AB$ and $BC$, and connect $E$ to $A$ and $C$. Working around the figure, one "proves" $AB = BC$.

*The flaw.* The proof assumes that $E$ lies *inside* the triangle and that the perpendicular feet lie *between* the vertices — i.e. it assumes an ordering of points on the sides. In a scalene triangle, the two lines meet *outside* the triangle, and the feet fall outside the segments. The equalities that the proof derives are still true, but they combine with different signs, and the conclusion does not follow.

*The general lesson.* The diagram encoded: (i) $E$ inside, (ii) feet between vertices, (iii) a particular order of the points on each side. None of these follows from the hypotheses. Hamming's observation applies: only by *meta-reasoning* — noticing that the conclusion is false — do we know the proof must be wrong, and then the search for the flaw begins.

**What to notice.** This is the most instructive false proof in geometry because the algebra is correct at every step. The error is entirely in the **case assumption** encoded in the figure. The defense is not "draw more carefully" — it is to derive the ordering from the hypotheses, or to prove the result for all orderings.

### Demonstration 32.4 — Boundary hunting on a theorem

*Theorem.* A continuous function on a closed bounded interval $[a,b]$ attains its maximum.

*Boundary hunt.* Which hypothesis can be deleted?

| Deleted hypothesis | Counterexample | What the hypothesis does |
|---|---|---|
| Continuity | $f(x) = x$ for $x<1$, $f(1)=0$ on $[0,1]$: no maximum (sup $1$ not attained) | Continuity prevents jumps that "miss" the supremum |
| Closedness | $f(x) = x$ on $(0,1)$: sup $1$, no maximum | Closedness keeps the limit point in the domain |
| Boundedness | $f(x) = x$ on $[0,\infty)$: unbounded | Boundedness prevents escape to infinity |

*Sharpness.* On $[0,1]$, $f(x)=x$ attains its maximum at $x=1$; the theorem is sharp in the sense that the maximum can be at an endpoint, so no stronger statement (e.g. "attained in the interior") holds.

**What to notice.** The table is the *complete* boundary analysis: every hypothesis is necessary, and each has a specific counterexample showing exactly what it prevents. **This table is a better understanding of the theorem than the proof alone**, because it tells you what happens in every neighboring theorem. When you learn a theorem, build this table.

## 5. Connections

- **← Ch. 4.** Counterexamples are the audit tool for proofs; the missing case is a boundary.
- **← Ch. 10.** Minimal counterexamples are descent; well-ordering guarantees they exist.
- **← Ch. 18.** Hypothesis deletion is the method for finding the right generality.
- **← Ch. 20.** Sharpness is the counterexample side of an optimization bound.
- **→ Ch. 31.** Every conjecture should be hunted before it is believed.
- **→ Ch. 33.** Systematic search is how counterexamples are found when they are not obvious.

## 6. Limitations

**A failed search is not a proof.** Not finding a counterexample in the cases you tried is weak evidence. The Pólya conjecture survived hundreds of millions of cases.

**Counterexamples can be non-constructive.** Some refutations show that a counterexample exists without producing one (e.g. by a cardinality or compactness argument). These are valid but less informative.

**The minimal counterexample may be enormous.** The minimal counterexample to a plausible conjecture can be far outside computational reach, which is why proofs are necessary even when the conjecture is "obviously" false.

**Sharpness may be unattainable.** Some bounds are not attained but are approached asymptotically; the correct statement is about a supremum, not a maximum (→ Ch. 11).

**Diagrams are necessary and dangerous.** Geometry without figures is impractical; geometry with figures is unreliable. The discipline is: use the figure to find the argument, use the algebra to verify the cases.

## 7. Applications

- **Mathematics.** Counterexamples are the standard tool for sharpening theorems; the history of analysis is full of them (Weierstrass's nowhere-differentiable function, Cantor's set, the Banach–Tarski paradox).
- **Software testing.** A test case is a search for a counterexample to "the program is correct"; boundary values and degenerate inputs are the highest-yield tests.
- **Engineering.** Failure analysis is boundary hunting: find the operating point where the design fails, and understand which assumption it violates.
- **Law.** A hypothetical is a counterexample to a rule; the reasoning that distinguishes it is the reasoning that limits the rule's scope.
- **Science.** A single anomalous observation can refute a theory; the anomaly is the counterexample, and its structure is the clue to the better theory.
- **Statistics.** Outliers and edge cases are the empirical counterexamples; investigating them is how models are improved (→ Ch. 29).

You have this chapter when you can:

1. Delete each hypothesis of a theorem in turn and produce a counterexample or a proof without it.
2. Build a boundary table for a theorem, as in Demonstration 32.4.
3. Find the smallest counterexample to a plausible claim and explain its structure.
4. Distinguish sharp from non-sharp bounds, and exhibit the extremal case.
5. Detect a case assumption hidden in a diagram, and repair the proof by deriving the ordering.
6. Use a counterexample to identify the missing hypothesis and state the corrected theorem.

**Diagnostic question.** "This theorem has three hypotheses. Which one is doing the work?" A learner who deletes them one at a time and produces counterexamples has the method.

### AI Tutor Brief

- **Generate:** (i) plausible false claims with a small counterexample; (ii) theorems to be boundary-tested (delete each hypothesis); (iii) false proofs with a hidden case assumption; (iv) sharpness questions for bounds.
- **Probe misconceptions:** "no counterexample found, so it's true"; a diagram as a case analysis; sharpness as an afterthought.
- **Transfer distance:** high. Test in algebra, analysis, combinatorics, and geometry.
- **Do not** accept "it's obviously true" for any universal claim. Require the boundary hunt.

# Chapter 33 — Mathematical Experimentation

## 1. Core Idea

**Experimental mathematics** is the systematic use of computation and structured exploration to discover, test, and refine mathematical claims. It is a laboratory discipline with its own methodology:

1. **Design the experiment.** Which parameter varies? What is measured? What would confirm or refute the hypothesis?
2. **Enumerate systematically.** Not the cases that are convenient — the cases that span the space.
3. **Tabulate and transform.** Differences, ratios, logs, residues. The right transform reveals the structure.
4. **Conjecture and test.** Propose the pattern, then test it on the *next* case, not the cases you used.
5. **Record everything**, including failures. The negative results are the most informative.

The output of an experiment is not a proof. It is a **conjecture with evidence**, and the evidence must be strong enough to justify the effort of proving it.

**Principle signature.** You have a computable family, a parameter to vary, and no formula. Or you have a formula and want to know whether it is sharp, general, or optimal.

## 2. First Principles

**Finite differences reveal polynomial structure.** If $a_n$ is a polynomial of degree $d$ in $n$, then the $d$-th differences are constant. Tabulating successive differences is the standard way to guess a polynomial, and the degree of the difference table is the degree of the polynomial.

**Ratios reveal exponential and factorial structure.** If $a_{n+1}/a_n$ tends to a constant, the sequence is asymptotically geometric; if it grows linearly, there is a factorial factor. Logs convert multiplicative structure to additive structure (→ Ch. 22).

**Residues reveal arithmetic structure.** Reducing a sequence modulo small primes reveals periodicity and divisibility patterns that are invisible in the raw values. Most number-theoretic conjectures are found this way.

**Search is only as good as the search space.** Enumerating $n = 1,\dots,20$ tests a tiny corner. A good experiment varies the *structure* (different graphs, different primes, different dimensions), not just the index.

**Confirmation bias is the main hazard.** You will notice the patterns you are looking for. The defense is to state the conjecture *before* testing it on new cases, and to test on cases chosen to break it.

## 3. Reasoning Process

1. **Define the family and the parameter.** What varies? What is fixed?
2. **Compute small cases by hand** to check the computation itself. (An experiment on a buggy program is an experiment on the program.)
3. **Automate the enumeration** over a structured range.
4. **Transform the data.** Differences, ratios, logs, residues, generating function coefficients.
5. **State the conjecture**, with its domain and its sharpness claim.
6. **Test on held-out cases**, especially adversarial ones.
7. **Look for the mechanism** — a counting argument, a recurrence, a bijection. Without a mechanism, the conjecture is a coincidence (→ Ch. 31).
8. **Record the failures**, and what they ruled out.

## 4. Demonstrations

### Demonstration 33.1 — Discovering an identity by tabulation

*Experiment.* Compute $S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} k\cdot k!$ for small $n$:

| $n$ | $S_n$ | $S_n + 1$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $1$ | $2 = 2!$ |
| 2 | $1 + 4 = 5$ | $6 = 3!$ |
| 3 | $5 + 18 = 23$ | $24 = 4!$ |
| 4 | $23 + 96 = 119$ | $120 = 5!$ |

*Conjecture.* $S_n = (n+1)! - 1$.

*Mechanism (and proof).* The summand telescopes:
$$k\cdot k! = \big((k+1)-1\big)k! = (k+1)! - k!.$$
Summing from $k=1$ to $n$:
$$\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big((k+1)! - k!\big) = (n+1)! - 1!. \qquad \blacksquare$$

**What to notice.** The table suggested the formula; the *mechanism* — telescoping — proved it and explained it. The mechanism was found by asking "why would $k\cdot k!$ telescope?", and the answer is that $k = (k+1)-1$ is exactly the difference that produces consecutive factorials.

**The general lesson.** When a table suggests a closed form, look for a **telescoping** structure first. It is the most common mechanism behind sums of the form $\sum f(k)$ with a factorial or binomial flavor, and it is the reason generating functions and finite differences are related (→ Ch. 15).

### Demonstration 33.2 — Discovering a counterexample by search

*Conjecture (false).* $2^n - 1$ is prime for all $n \ge 2$.

*Search.* Compute $2^n - 1$ and test primality for small $n$:

| $n$ | $2^n-1$ | Prime? |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 3 | yes |
| 3 | 7 | yes |
| 4 | 15 | no ($3\cdot5$) |
| 5 | 31 | yes |
| 6 | 63 | no |
| 7 | 127 | yes |
| 8 | 255 | no |
| 9 | 511 | no ($7\cdot73$) |
| 10 | 1023 | no |
| 11 | 2047 | **no** ($23\cdot89$) |

*Observation.* For composite $n$, $2^n-1$ factors (since $2^{ab}-1$ is divisible by $2^a-1$). So the interesting cases are prime $n$. But $n=11$ is prime and $2^{11}-1 = 2047 = 23\cdot89$ is composite.

*Repair.* The correct conjecture is about **Mersenne primes**: $2^p - 1$ is prime for *some* primes $p$ (2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, ...), not all. The search found the first failure at $p=11$, which is close enough to be found by hand and far enough to be missed by casual checking.

**What to notice.** The search was structured: the parity/compositeness observation reduced the search space to prime $n$, and the failure appeared immediately. **Good experiments reduce the search space by structure before enumerating.** Brute force over all $n$ would have found the same counterexample at $n=4$ (a composite case, less informative); the structural reduction found the *interesting* one.

### Demonstration 33.3 — Finite differences and the polynomial guess

*Experiment.* Count the maximum number of regions into which $n$ lines in general position divide the plane:

| $n$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $R_n$ | 1 | 2 | 4 | 7 | 11 | 16 |

*First differences:* $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$.
*Second differences:* $1, 1, 1, 1$ — constant.

*Conjecture.* $R_n$ is a quadratic polynomial in $n$. With second difference $1$, the leading coefficient is $1/2$. Fitting $R_n = \frac12 n^2 + bn + c$ to $R_0 = 1$, $R_1 = 2$ gives $c = 1$ and $\frac12 + b + 1 = 2$, so $b = \frac12$. Hence
$$R_n = \frac{n^2+n+2}{2} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} + 1.$$
Check $n=2$: $3+1 = 4$ ✓; $n=5$: $15+1 = 16$ ✓.

*Mechanism.* Adding the $n$-th line: it crosses the previous $n-1$ lines at $n-1$ points, dividing it into $n$ segments, each of which splits an existing region. So $R_n = R_{n-1} + n$, with $R_0 = 1$. Summing gives $R_n = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^n k = 1 + n(n+1)/2$. $\blacksquare$

**What to notice.** The finite-difference table found the *degree* and the leading coefficient, which is enough to fit the rest. The recurrence found the *mechanism*, which gives the proof and generalizes (to hyperplanes, to circles, to other arrangements).

**The division of labor is the point:** the table is fast and unreliable; the mechanism is slow and certain. Use the table to guess, the mechanism to prove, and always do both.

### Demonstration 33.4 — The experiment that lies

*Cautionary case.* The Pólya conjecture (1919): for every $n \ge 2$, at least half of the integers $2,\dots,n$ have an odd number of prime factors (counted with multiplicity). It was verified for all $n$ up to hundreds of millions, and it is **false**: a counterexample was found (by Haselgrove, 1958) near $9\times10^8$.

*Why the experiment was misleading.* The conjecture is about a *sign change* in a sum that is dominated by a slowly varying main term; the sign change occurs only after the fluctuations accumulate past the main term. The first counterexample is far beyond any feasible hand computation, and even the early computer searches missed it.

**What to notice.** The evidence was overwhelming by the standards of any practical experiment, and the conjecture was still false. **Finite verification cannot establish a universal statement**, no matter how many cases (→ Ch. 4). This is not a reason to stop experimenting; it is a reason to treat experiments as *evidence about mechanism*, not as proof. The experiment's real value was in suggesting the mechanism (the distribution of $\omega(n)$), which is what eventually led to the proof of the related theorems.

## 5. Connections

- **← Ch. 12.** Counting sequences are the most common experimental objects.
- **← Ch. 22.** Asymptotic fitting is the transform that reveals growth structure.
- **← Ch. 28.** Computational methodology (verification, error) is the infrastructure of experimentation.
- **← Ch. 31.** Conjecture formation is the output of the experiment.
- **→ Ch. 32.** The experiment's most valuable output is a counterexample.
- **→ Ch. 35.** Knowing when to stop experimenting and start proving is a judgment call.

## 6. Limitations

**Experiments cannot prove universals.** No finite number of cases establishes "for all $n$". This is not a technicality; it is the definition of a universal quantifier.

**The search space is usually wrong.** Enumerating indices tests a thin slice; the structure that matters may be in a different dimension (larger primes, different graphs, higher dimension).

**Computational bugs produce mathematical "discoveries".** An experiment on a buggy program discovers a property of the program. Verification against hand-computed cases is mandatory.

**Patterns can be coincidences.** The Moser circle problem (→ Ch. 31) is the canonical example: five cases agreeing is not evidence of a mechanism.

**Recording is undervalued.** The negative results — the cases that failed, the patterns that broke — are the most informative and the most often lost. Keep a log.

## 7. Applications

- **Mathematics.** The discovery of the Rogers–Ramanujan identities, the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, and countless combinatorial identities came from systematic computation.
- **Number theory.** Primality patterns, class numbers, and $L$-function zeros are explored computationally before being proved.
- **Combinatorics.** Enumerating small cases is how most conjectures about graphs, permutations, and partitions are born.
- **Physics.** Experimental mathematics overlaps with numerical simulation; the difference is that the target is a theorem, not a measurement.
- **Machine learning.** Automated conjecture generation (pattern finding in sequences, symbolic regression) is an active research area; the methodology is the one in this chapter, automated.
- **Engineering.** Design-space exploration is structured experimentation; the finite-difference and scaling techniques apply to performance data.

## 8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

1. Design an experiment that varies structure, not just index.
2. Use finite differences, ratios, logs, and residues to transform data into a recognizable form.
3. State a conjecture from data, with a mechanism, before testing it on new cases.
4. Test adversarially on held-out and boundary cases.
5. Recognize when a pattern is a coincidence and when it has a mechanism.
6. Keep a record of negative results and use them to constrain the next experiment.

**Diagnostic question.** "You have a table of values. What do you do next?" A learner who transforms the data and looks for a mechanism has the method. A learner who stares at the numbers does not.

### AI Tutor Brief

- **Generate:** (i) sequences to be identified by differences/ratios; (ii) families to be enumerated with a structural reduction; (iii) patterns with a hidden failure just outside the tested range; (iv) "design the experiment" tasks where the learner must choose what to vary.
- **Probe misconceptions:** "verified for many cases, therefore true"; brute force over indices as a substitute for structural variation; ignoring the possibility of a computational bug.
- **Transfer distance:** high. Use sequences, graphs, and geometric configurations.
- **Do not** accept a conjecture from data without a mechanism and a held-out test.

# Chapter 34 — Problem Invention and Original Approaches

## 1. Core Idea

Originality in mathematics is usually not the invention of a new subject. It is the **transformation of an existing problem** into one that is new, tractable, and interesting. The transformations are systematic:

| Operation | Question | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Generalize** | What if $2$ becomes $k$? | Adds structure, may reveal the mechanism |
| **Specialize** | What is the simplest case? | Reveals the core difficulty |
| **Invert** | Swap the given and the sought | Often converts a hard forward problem into an easy inverse one |
| **Compose** | What if we combine two problems? | Produces new structure at the interface |
| **Dualize** | What is the dual statement? | Doubles the problem space |
| **Weaken** | What if the conclusion is weaker? | May become provable, and reveals what was hard |
| **Strengthen** | What if the conclusion is stronger? | May become easier (→ Ch. 10, 18) |
| **Negate** | What if the claim is false? | Produces a counterexample hunt (→ Ch. 32) |
| **Change the question** | What should we be asking instead? | The highest-value and rarest move |

**Principle signature.** You have solved a problem and want to know what it was really about; or you have a problem you cannot solve and want a related one you can; or you are looking for a research direction.

## 2. First Principles

**A problem is a constraint system plus a goal** (→ Ch. 7). Transforming any component produces a new problem. The art is choosing a transformation that preserves the *interesting* structure and changes the *hard* structure.

**Inversion is the most underused operation.** "Given the configuration, find the number" becomes "given the number, find the configuration"; "given the input, find the output" becomes "given the output, find an input"; "prove this theorem" becomes "characterize the cases where it fails". Inversions often convert an existence problem into a classification problem, or a computation into a search — and one of the two is usually tractable.

Hamming's account of his own research is the model: faced with "I need an acre of programmers", he inverted to "make the machine do the programming", which turned a resource constraint into a research program. The inversion did not solve the problem; it *changed which problem he had*.

**Strengthening is the second most underused operation.** Proving a stronger theorem is often easier because the stronger statement has more structure (→ Ch. 10, 18). "For all $n$" may be harder than "for all $n$, with an explicit error term", because the error term constrains the proof.

**Problem invention is how research begins.** Most research problems are not found; they are constructed by transforming a known result until a gap appears. The gap is the research problem.

**Taste is the selection criterion.** Among the infinitely many transformed problems, the good ones are:
- **Sharp:** the answer is exact, not approximate, and the bound is attained.
- **Surprising:** the answer is not what the naive approach predicts.
- **Connected:** the problem links two areas, so progress transfers.
- **Short to state:** the statement fits in a paragraph, even if the proof does not.

## 3. Reasoning Process

1. **State the solved problem in its most general form.** What is the actual theorem?
2. **Apply each transformation** in the table, and write down the resulting problem in one sentence.
3. **Filter by tractability.** Which transformed problems have a chance? (Known techniques, small cases, existing literature.)
4. **Filter by interest.** Which have a sharp answer, a surprise, or a connection?
5. **Attempt the survivors.** Even a failed attempt tells you whether the problem is deep.
6. **For a written problem, design it backwards from the key step.** Choose the principle you want to test, construct a situation where it is the only way through, and disguise the situation.

## 4. Demonstrations

### Demonstration 34.1 — Nine problems from one

*Starting point.* The identity $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} 1/k^2 = \pi^2/6$.

Apply the transformations:

1. **Generalize the exponent:** $\sum 1/k^s = \zeta(s)$ for $s > 1$. *Result:* the Riemann zeta function — a whole subject.
2. **Specialize to odd terms:** $\sum_{k \text{ odd}} 1/k^2 = \frac{\pi^2}{8}$. *Result:* a tractable variant, provable by subtracting the even terms.
3. **Alternate the signs:** $\sum (-1)^{k+1}/k^2 = \frac{\pi^2}{12}$. *Result:* the Dirichlet eta function, related to $\zeta$ by a simple factor.
4. **Restrict the sum:** $\sum_{k=1}^{N} 1/k^2$ — no closed form, but asymptotically $\pi^2/6 - 1/N$. *Result:* an asymptotic problem (→ Ch. 22).
5. **Invert:** given a value $v$, for which $s$ is $\zeta(s) = v$? *Result:* the inverse zeta problem, which is hard and interesting.
6. **Compose with a character:** $\sum \chi(k)/k^2$ for a Dirichlet character $\chi$. *Result:* Dirichlet $L$-functions, and the proof of Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions.
7. **Dualize (product form):** $\zeta(s) = \prod_p (1-p^{-s})^{-1}$. *Result:* the Euler product, which connects the sum to the primes — the single most important structural fact in the subject.
8. **Weaken:** prove only that the sum converges. *Result:* an easy exercise (comparison with $\int 1/x^2$).
9. **Strengthen:** prove the exact value with an explicit error term for the partial sums. *Result:* the Basel problem with a rate, which requires the Euler–Maclaurin formula.

**What to notice.** One identity generated nine problems of wildly different difficulty and interest. Three are exercises, three are theorems, and three are research programs. **The transformations are mechanical; the selection is the skill.** This is what "finding a research problem" looks like in practice: generate many variants, and choose the one whose difficulty is in the interesting place.

### Demonstration 34.2 — Inversion

*Original problem.* "Given a graph, determine whether it has a Hamiltonian cycle." This is hard (NP-complete).

*Inversion 1.* "Given a number $n$, what is the smallest graph on $n$ vertices with no Hamiltonian cycle and minimum degree $d$?" This is a construction problem, and it is tractable: the two-triangles-plus-bridge graph (→ Ch. 32) answers it for $d=2$.

*Inversion 2.* "Characterize the graphs that *do* have a Hamiltonian cycle." This is the classification form, and it is the form in which sufficient conditions (Dirac, Ore) were found.

*Inversion 3.* "Given a Hamiltonian cycle, what graphs contain it?" This is trivial, and its triviality is informative: it shows that the hard direction is the *existence* question, not the verification.

**What to notice.** The four versions of "the same problem" have completely different difficulty profiles. The original is NP-complete; the first inversion is a small construction; the second is a research program; the third is trivial. **Inversion does not make the problem easier — it changes which problem you are solving**, and the skill is choosing the version whose difficulty matches the tools you have.

### Demonstration 34.3 — Designing a problem backwards from a principle

*Goal.* Write a problem whose only clean solution uses the **coloring invariant** (→ Ch. 8).

*Step 1: choose the invariant.* A chessboard coloring, with the property that every tile covers one black and one white square.

*Step 2: choose the region so the invariant obstructs.* The region must have unequal black and white counts. The simplest such region is a board with two same-colored corners removed.

*Step 3: choose the tile so the invariant is sharp.* Dominoes ($1\times2$) cover one of each. If the tile were a tromino, the invariant would not apply.

*Step 4: disguise the setup.* Instead of a chessboard, use a $6\times6$ board with two opposite corners removed and ask for a tiling by $1\times2$ dominoes. The numbers $6$ and "opposite corners" are chosen so the parity computation is not immediate.

*Step 5: verify the problem is not solvable by counting.* The area is $34$, which is even, so the naive area count does not obstruct. The invariant is the only obstruction, which is exactly the property a good problem has: **the obvious approach fails, and the intended principle succeeds.**

**What to notice.** The problem was *constructed* from the solution, not discovered. This is how olympiad problems are made, and it is how research problems are made too: start from a technique, find the situation where it is the only way through, and then present the situation without the technique. **Writing a problem is a way of understanding a principle at a depth that solving cannot reach**, because you must know exactly which features are load-bearing (→ Ch. 2).

### Demonstration 34.4 — Evaluating candidate problems

*Candidates.*

**(a)** "Prove that every finite graph has an even number of odd-degree vertices." — Sharp (the statement is exact), not surprising (it is a standard corollary), connected (it links to Eulerian circuits), short. *Verdict:* a good exercise, a poor research problem.

**(b)** "Determine the maximum number of edges in a graph on $n$ vertices with no triangle." — Sharp (Turán's theorem gives the exact answer), surprising (the extremal graph is complete bipartite, not a random sparse graph), connected (extremal graph theory, Ramsey theory), short. *Verdict:* an excellent problem — and indeed a famous theorem.

**(c)** "Find a formula for the number of primes less than $x$." — Sharp (there is no elementary closed form, and the precise asymptotics is the prime number theorem), surprising, connected, short. *Verdict:* an excellent problem, but the difficulty is at the research level, so it is a poor *training* problem.

**What to notice.** The four criteria — sharpness, surprise, connection, brevity — do not measure difficulty. They measure *quality*. A problem can be excellent and easy (a), excellent and hard (b), or excellent and intractable (c). Choosing among them is a matter of the learner's level and the purpose of the problem, and that choice is taste.

## 5. Connections

- **← Ch. 7.** Problem anatomy is the input to transformation: you cannot transform what you have not analyzed.
- **← Ch. 18, 19.** Generalization and duality are two of the transformations in the table.
- **← Ch. 31.** Conjecture formation is problem invention applied to a claim rather than a question.
- **← Ch. 33.** Experimentation generates the data that suggest which variant is worth pursuing.
- **→ Ch. 35.** Choosing which invented problem to pursue is the central act of research judgment.

## 6. Limitations

**Most transformations produce uninteresting problems.** The yield is low, and that is normal. The skill is in generating many and discarding most, not in generating one perfect variant.

**Inversion can produce a problem you cannot recognize.** The inverted problem may be in a different field, with different tools. This is an advantage if you can bridge the fields, and a dead end if you cannot.

**Strengthening can make a problem false.** "Stronger" is not "better" if the stronger statement is not true. Test the strengthened statement on small cases before investing.

**Problem invention can become avoidance.** Generating variants is easier than solving any of them. Set a rule: generate at most five variants, then commit to one.

**Taste is not teachable by rule.** The four criteria (sharpness, surprise, connection, brevity) are descriptive, not algorithmic. Taste improves by exposure to many good and bad problems, and by writing problems and seeing which ones others find interesting.

## 7. Applications

- **Research.** Most papers begin with a transformation of a known result; the "novelty" is the transformation, not the technique.
- **Teaching.** Designing problems backwards from a principle is how good curricula are built (→ the AI Tutor Protocol).
- **Engineering.** Design problems are invented by transforming requirements; "what if we relax this constraint?" is the generalization operation.
- **Consulting.** Reframing a client's problem ("you asked for a faster algorithm; the real problem is the data model") is the "change the question" operation.
- **Writing.** Mathematical exposition is problem invention in reverse: you present the problem so that the solution is inevitable.
- **Art and design.** The transformations are the same: generalize, invert, compose, dualize. Originality is systematic, not magical.

## 8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

1. Apply all nine transformations to a given problem and state the resulting problems in one sentence each.
2. Choose a transformation that preserves the interesting structure and changes the hard structure.
3. Invert a problem and identify which version is tractable.
4. Design a problem backwards from a chosen principle, with the property that the obvious approach fails.
5. Evaluate a candidate problem on sharpness, surprise, connection, and brevity.
6. Generate at least three genuinely new problems from a single result.

**Diagnostic question.** "You have solved this. What else have you solved?" A learner who can generate variants has the inventive habit. A learner who stops at the answer does not.

### AI Tutor Brief

- **Generate:** (i) a result plus a required list of transformed variants; (ii) "design a problem" tasks with a specified principle and a required disguise; (iii) candidate problems to be evaluated on the four criteria; (iv) inversion tasks where the learner must identify the tractable version.
- **Probe misconceptions:** novelty as the goal (rather than interest); transformation without a purpose; generating variants as a substitute for solving.
- **Transfer distance:** maximal. Every task should require the learner to *create* something.
- **Do not** accept a variant that is merely a relabeling. Require a genuine change of structure.

# Chapter 35 — Research Judgment

## 1. Core Idea

Research judgment is the ability to make good decisions under uncertainty about which problems to pursue, when to persist, when to abandon, whom to trust, and what to believe. It is not a technique; it is a set of calibrated habits.

Four components:

1. **Problem choice.** The single highest-leverage decision. Working on an important problem badly beats working on an unimportant problem well.
2. **Persistence and abandonment.** Both are skills, and both require a criterion. Persistence without a criterion is stubbornness; abandonment without a criterion is dilettantism.
3. **Error correction.** The systematic detection of your own mistakes, before anyone else does.
4. **The long game.** Research compounds. Consistent direction over years produces results that no single burst can.

Hamming's framing is the right one: the question is not "how do I solve this problem?" but "what is the most important problem I could be working on, and am I actually working on it?".

## 2. First Principles

**Importance is a property of the problem, not of the effort.** A brilliant solution to a trivial problem is a trivial contribution. Importance is judged by: does it change what others can do? does it connect things? does it survive?

**Tractability is a property of the state of the field.** A problem that was hopeless in 1950 may be routine in 2020 because the tools arrived. Judgment includes knowing what tools now exist.

**Compounding requires direction.** If each step is in a random direction, the distance from the start after $n$ steps grows as $\sqrt n$ (the drunkard's walk). If each step is in a consistent direction, it grows as $n$. For $n = 1000$ steps, that is the difference between $32$ and $1000$ — a factor of $31$. **A vision of where you are going is worth more than any single step**, and the mathematics is not a metaphor; it is the actual scaling.

**Errors are the default.** Most research ideas are wrong. The productive response is not to avoid error but to *detect* it cheaply and early: state claims precisely, test them on small cases, and seek the strongest possible objection.

**Studying successes beats studying failures.** There are many ways to be wrong and few to be right. Learning the patterns of successful work (how the problem was chosen, how the key idea was found, how the error was caught) transfers better than a catalog of failures — though both matter.

**Tolerance for ambiguity is a prerequisite.** Research problems are ill-defined for a long time. People who need the problem to be clear before starting never start.

**Courage is a practical requirement.** The key step often looks unjustified until it works. Shannon's "average over all random codes" was a leap; so was Hamming's decision to use a metric that violated the obvious choice. Without the willingness to pursue a step that is not yet justified, the step is never taken.

## 3. Reasoning Process

1. **Maintain a list of important problems.** Five to ten, written down, revisited quarterly. The list is the instrument; without it, attention drifts to whatever is in front of you.
2. **For each, record: why it matters, what is known, what the obstruction is, what would count as progress.** A problem you cannot describe this way is not yet a research problem.
3. **Choose by importance × tractability × fit.** Fit includes your tools, your taste, and your tolerance for the kind of work involved.
4. **Work in bounded sessions with a written state.** What I know, what I want, what I tried, what failed. The written state is what makes resumption possible and prevents re-treading.
5. **Apply the error-correction protocol** (below) to every claim before believing it.
6. **At the checkpoint, decide:** progress, plateau, or dead end. Use a criterion, not a feeling.
7. **Review the list.** Is the problem still important? Has the field moved? Are you working on it, or on something adjacent that is easier?

### The error-correction protocol

1. **State the claim precisely** (→ Ch. 2, 3). Vague claims cannot be checked.
2. **Test the smallest case** and the degenerate case.
3. **Attempt the counterexample** (→ Ch. 32), deliberately.
4. **Identify the weakest step** and try to break it.
5. **Write the proof for an adversary** — someone who wants to find the gap.
6. **Wait 24 hours** and reread. Distance reveals what familiarity hides.
7. **Explain it to someone outside the field.** The question you cannot answer is the gap.

## 4. Demonstrations

### Demonstration 35.1 — The compounding argument, quantified

*Model.* Suppose you take $n = 1000$ "steps" of research effort, each of unit length.

- **Random direction:** the displacement after $n$ steps is a random walk, with expected distance $\sqrt n \approx 32$.
- **Consistent direction:** the displacement is $n = 1000$.

*Interpretation.* The ratio is $\sqrt n \approx 32$. With $10{,}000$ steps, the ratio is $100$. The advantage of direction grows with the length of the career.

*Caveat.* The model is a cartoon: research steps are not independent, and "consistent direction" is not always available (the field moves). But the qualitative conclusion is robust: **a coherent research program outperforms a sequence of disconnected projects by a factor that grows with time.** This is the mathematical form of Hamming's "vision" argument.

**What to notice.** The argument does not say "have a vision and you will succeed". It says: *if* you have a direction, the expected distance grows linearly rather than as a square root. The conditional is doing the work, and the condition is the hard part.

### Demonstration 35.2 — Applying the important-problem test

*Candidate directions for a graduate student in combinatorics.*

| Direction | Why it matters | Known | Obstruction | Progress would be |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A.** A new proof of a known theorem | Low: the theorem is established | Everything | None — the work is expository | A simpler proof, which may generalize |
| **B.** A conjecture with strong numerical evidence and no proof | High: many people care | The evidence, some special cases | No technique controls the error term | A proof in a special case, or a new technique |
| **C.** A new question at the interface of two areas | Uncertain: depends on the connection | Little | The definitions may not be compatible | A precise statement of the connection |
| **D.** A problem suggested by an application | Medium: depends on the application's importance | The applied literature | The mathematical structure may be absent | A model that is both faithful and tractable |

*Judgment.* B is the classic high-value choice: important, with a clear obstruction, where partial progress is meaningful. A is low-risk and low-yield. C is high-variance: it could be a new field or a dead end, and it requires a period of exploration before the problem is even well-posed. D is valuable only if the application is genuinely important and the structure is real.

**What to notice.** The table forces the questions that most people skip: *what is the obstruction?* and *what would count as progress?* A problem without a named obstruction is not yet a research problem; it is an interest.

### Demonstration 35.3 — The stuckness protocol

*Situation.* You have worked on a problem for three weeks with no progress.

*Protocol.*

1. **Write the state.** What is known, what is wanted, what has been tried, what failed and why. (The "why" is the valuable part.)
2. **Ask the form question.** If I had a solution, what would it look like? (→ Ch. 7, 17)
3. **Ask the assumption question.** Which hypothesis have I not used? Which one am I using implicitly? (→ Ch. 7, 6)
4. **Change the problem.** Generalize, specialize, invert (→ Ch. 34). Work on a variant for a week.
5. **Incubate deliberately.** Set the problem aside, work on something else, and let the subconscious process it. (Hamming's observation: the temporary abandonment is a common feature of creative work.)
6. **Set a criterion for abandonment.** "If I cannot name the obstruction after another two weeks, I will write up what I have learned and switch."

**What to notice.** Every step is a *technique*, not a mood. Stuckness is not a signal to try harder; it is a signal to change the representation, the problem, or the level of abstraction. **The only non-technique is "persist without changing anything", and it is the one most people choose.**

### Demonstration 35.4 — Error correction in practice

*Claim.* "For every graph $G$, the chromatic number is at most the maximum degree plus one."

*Protocol.*
1. **Precise?** Yes: $\chi(G) \le \Delta(G) + 1$.
2. **Smallest case:** $K_1$: $\chi=1$, $\Delta=0$, $1 \le 1$ ✓. $K_2$: $\chi=2$, $\Delta=1$, $2 \le 2$ ✓.
3. **Degenerate case:** the empty graph on $n$ vertices: $\chi = 1$, $\Delta = 0$, $1 \le 1$ ✓.
4. **Counterexample hunt:** the greedy argument gives $\Delta+1$ (each vertex has at most $\Delta$ neighbors, so among $\Delta+1$ colors one is free). The bound is attained by odd cycles: $C_5$ has $\chi=3$, $\Delta=2$, so $3 \le 3$ ✓. And by $K_{\Delta+1}$: $\chi = \Delta+1$ ✓. So the bound is sharp.
5. **Weakest step:** the greedy argument assumes a vertex ordering; it works for any ordering, so the step is robust.
6. **Adversarial reading:** "at most $\Delta+1$" — can it be improved to $\Delta$? No: odd cycles and complete graphs show it cannot. The bound is sharp, and the theorem is correct as stated.

*Outcome.* The claim survives all six checks, and the checks have produced additional information: the bound is sharp, and the extremal graphs are odd cycles and complete graphs. **Error correction is not just a filter; it is a source of understanding.**

**What to notice.** The protocol took ten minutes and produced a stronger result than the original claim (sharpness plus the extremal examples). This is typical: **the checks that catch errors are the same checks that reveal structure.** A claim that survives a serious audit is a claim you understand.

- **← Ch. 4.** The error-correction protocol is the audit of Chapter 4 applied to your own research claims.
- **← Ch. 31.** Conjecture formation is the input to problem choice; a conjecture with a mechanism is a research program.
- **← Ch. 34.** Problem invention generates the candidates that judgment selects among.
- **← Ch. 24.** Strategy under pressure is the same selection problem at a shorter timescale.
- **→ All.** Judgment is the integration of everything else; it is what remains when no signature table applies.

## 6. Limitations

**Judgment is not algorithmic.** The criteria (importance, tractability, fit) are heuristics, and they conflict. There is no procedure that produces the right choice; there is only the disciplined application of the criteria plus experience.

**Importance is often visible only in retrospect.** The most important problems of a generation are frequently not recognized as such at the time. This is not a reason to ignore importance; it is a reason to be humble about your estimates of it.

**The compounding argument assumes a direction that may not exist.** Fields move, tools change, and a direction that was coherent at the start may be obsolete at the end. The model is a cartoon, not a guarantee.

**Persistence can be a trap.** The sunk-cost fallacy is real, and the people most likely to fall into it are the most capable (they have succeeded before, so they believe they will succeed again). The criterion for abandonment is the defense.

**Studying successes has survivorship bias.** The successful researchers who are studied are the ones who succeeded; the ones who followed the same strategy and failed are invisible (→ Ch. 29). The lessons are real but the sample is selected.

## 7. Applications

- **Academic research.** Problem choice, grant strategy, collaboration, and the management of a multi-year program.
- **Industry research.** Choosing projects with a real chance of mattering, and knowing when to kill them.
- **Engineering leadership.** The same judgment applied to technical directions: which bets are worth making, and what would count as evidence that a bet is failing.
- **Any long-horizon project.** The compounding argument applies to any skill or program: consistent direction dominates scattered effort.
- **Personal development.** The choice of what to become good at is the same problem as the choice of what to research, with the same criteria.

## 8. Mastery Criteria

You have this chapter when you can:

1. Maintain a written list of important problems with the four fields (why, known, obstruction, progress) filled in.
2. Apply the important-problem test to a candidate direction and articulate the obstruction.
3. Use a written stuckness protocol with an explicit abandonment criterion.
4. Apply the seven-step error-correction protocol to a claim of your own, and report what the audit revealed.
5. Explain the compounding argument and its assumptions.
6. Distinguish persistence from stubbornness, and abandonment from dilettantism, by naming the criterion in each case.

**Diagnostic question.** "What are you working on, why does it matter, and what is the obstruction?" A learner who can answer all three has research judgment. A learner who can answer only the first has an interest.

### AI Tutor Brief

- **Generate:** (i) research-direction scenarios to be evaluated with the important-problem table; (ii) stuckness scenarios requiring a protocol, not a solution; (iii) claims to be audited with the seven-step protocol; (iv) prioritization problems where the learner must choose among five projects with stated criteria.
- **Probe misconceptions:** effort as a substitute for importance; persistence without a criterion; the sunk-cost fallacy; treating a selected sample of successes as evidence.
- **Transfer distance:** maximal. These are life decisions, not exercises.
- **Do not** grade the *choice* of problem. Grade the *reasoning*: are the criteria explicit, is the obstruction named, is the abandonment criterion stated?
